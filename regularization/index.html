Regularization

The Art of Constraint: why paying a toll on coefficient size makes a model smarter

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression

In the previous article we watched a polynomial go feral: give a model enough flexibility and too little data, and it stops learning the pattern and starts memorizing the noise. We diagnosed the disease. This article is about the cure, and the cure is stranger than it sounds. We are going to make the model worse at its job on purpose, and it is going to get better.


Here is the intuition. Imagine hiring someone and telling them: "I don't care how you do it, just hit the number." You will get the number, and you will also get creative accounting, wild swings, and a strategy nobody can repeat next quarter. Ordinary least squares is exactly that boss. Its only instruction is minimize the squared error, so when you hand it fifteen knobs and twenty data points, it will happily crank every knob to an absurd setting if that shaves one more decimal off the training error.


Regularization adds the missing half of the instruction: "hit the number, but keep your methods simple." Formally, we stop minimizing error alone and start minimizing error plus a toll on complexity:

$$ \text{Total Loss} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{prediction error}}_{\text{how wrong}} \;+\; \lambda \cdot \underbrace{\text{coefficient size}}_{\text{how extreme}} $$

Every coefficient now has to pay rent. A feature can still take a large weight, but only if the error it removes is worth more than the toll it owes. The parameter \(\lambda\) sets the tax rate: at \(\lambda = 0\) we are back to unconstrained least squares, and as \(\lambda\) grows the model is squeezed toward saying nothing at all.


Where does this show up? Everywhere the feature count is uncomfortable compared to the sample count: genomics with 20,000 genes and 200 patients, text models with a vocabulary of a million tokens, credit scoring with hundreds of engineered ratios that all say roughly the same thing. In those rooms, an unregularized linear model is not a baseline, it is a liability. Let's build one and break it.

Growing a Monster

Twenty noisy observations of a simple wave, \(y = \sin(x) + x/2\). We hand the model fifteen polynomial features to work with, which is far more flexibility than twenty points can possibly justify. The dashed line is the truth we are trying to recover. The bars underneath the chart are the coefficients themselves, on a logarithmic scale, because the most damning evidence of overfitting is not the shape of the curve. It is the arithmetic.

The Target

Twenty dots, sampled from the dashed curve and then jostled by noise. A good model recovers the dashed line. A bad model recovers the jostling. Nothing about the dots tells you which is which, and that is the whole difficulty.

Unleash Fifteen Degrees

Fit a degree-15 polynomial with no constraints at all. It threads itself through the data beautifully, and between the points it convulses. Training error is nearly perfect. Ask it about any x it has not seen and the answer is somewhere between wrong and deranged.

The Smoking Gun

Look at the bars. To whip a curve up and down that violently, the polynomial terms must fight each other with enormous, nearly cancelling weights. The largest has grown past 900, on features that were scaled to standard deviation 1. No honest process has coefficients like that. Coefficient size is the fingerprint of overfitting, and that is precisely what we are about to tax.

Charge Rent

Now add a small penalty on the sum of squared coefficients. Nothing else changes: same data, same fifteen features, same solver. The largest weight collapses roughly a hundredfold and the curve stops convulsing. We paid a little training error and bought back an enormous amount of sanity.

Closer to the Truth

Turn the tax rate up a little further and we reach the best this data allows. Measured against the dashed line, error has fallen from 0.82 to 0.17, even though the model now misses almost every dot it used to hit. This is the whole bargain: deliberately fit the data you have a bit worse, in exchange for fitting the world a lot better.

Overdose

Push the penalty too far and the cure becomes the disease. Every coefficient is squeezed toward zero, the curve flattens into a near-horizontal line, and the model has been taxed into silence. It is no longer overfitting, it is refusing to say anything at all. \(\lambda\) is a dial, not a switch.

Two Ways to Charge Rent

We taxed the coefficients, but we never said how to measure their size. That choice turns out to matter enormously. There are two natural answers, and they produce models with completely different personalities.


Ridge (also called L2 or Tikhonov regularization) charges the sum of squared coefficients:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j} \beta_j^2 $$

Squaring makes large weights disproportionately expensive. A coefficient of 10 costs 100; a coefficient of 100 costs 10,000. The model becomes desperate to keep every weight small. And because the penalty is smooth, Ridge has the same luxury OLS had: a closed-form solution, which is just the Normal Equation with one extra term bolted onto the diagonal.

$$ \hat{\beta}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^{\top}X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^{\top} y $$

That single \(+\lambda I\) is doing something quietly profound. Remember from the previous article that \(X^{\top}X\) becomes uninvertible when features are redundant. Adding \(\lambda\) to the diagonal makes it invertible again, always. Ridge does not just reduce variance, it repairs a broken matrix.


A digression that is really a warning, and it bit this article during construction. Write that formula literally, forming \(X^{\top}X\) and inverting it, and you square the condition number of your problem. On the degree-15 polynomial above, the design matrix is already borderline, so squaring it pushes the arithmetic past what double precision can represent and the "unpenalized" fit quietly comes back wrong. Every model on this page is therefore solved by QR decomposition instead, which never forms \(X^{\top}X\) at all. There is a satisfying trick behind it: stacking \(\sqrt{\lambda}I\) underneath \(X\), and zeros underneath \(y\), turns Ridge into an ordinary least squares problem. You are literally inventing fake observations that all insist the coefficients should be zero, which is an honest description of what a penalty is. It is also why the extreme coefficient values you may see quoted for fits like this one should be treated with suspicion: below a certain \(\lambda\), two correct solvers stop agreeing with each other.


Lasso (L1, and an acronym for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) charges the sum of absolute coefficients instead:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{lasso}} = \sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j} |\beta_j| $$

A one-character change, and the consequences are dramatic. Squaring is forgiving near zero: shrinking a coefficient from 0.02 to 0.01 saves almost nothing, so Ridge never quite bothers finishing the job. Absolute value is not forgiving. The saving is the same whether you move from 10.01 to 10.00 or from 0.01 to 0.00, so Lasso keeps pushing until coefficients land on exactly zero and the feature is gone from the model entirely. Ridge shrinks. Lasso selects.


Below, both penalties attack the same overgrown polynomial. Drag the tax rate and watch two different philosophies at work: the blue curve keeps all fifteen features on a tight leash, while the pink one fires most of them.

Error vs truth is measured against the noiseless curve, not the noisy dots, which is the score we actually care about and the one no real dataset lets you compute.

Why a Diamond Deletes

"Absolute value is not forgiving near zero" is a decent story, but it is not the real explanation. The real explanation is geometric, and it is the single most illuminating picture in this corner of statistics.


Start with a change of view. Minimizing "error plus penalty" is equivalent to a different problem: minimize error alone, subject to a budget on total coefficient size.

$$ \min_{\beta} \sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|\beta\| \le t $$

Every \(\lambda\) in the penalized form corresponds to some budget \(t\) in this constrained form, and vice versa. Turning the tax rate up is the same as tightening the belt. This matters because the constrained version can be drawn.


With two coefficients, the whole optimization lives on a flat plane. Squared error becomes a bowl centered on the OLS solution, and slicing that bowl at fixed heights gives ellipses of equal error: every point on one ring fits the training data exactly as well as every other point on it. The budget is a region around the origin. The best legal model sits where the smallest ellipse first touches the budget region.


And now the punchline. The L2 budget \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le t\) is a circle. A circle is smooth everywhere, so an expanding ellipse will kiss it at some generic point, with both coordinates nonzero. The L1 budget \(|\beta_1| + |\beta_2| \le t\) is a diamond, and a diamond has corners, and its corners sit exactly on the axes, where one coefficient is zero. Corners stick out, so they get touched first. That is the entire mechanism behind feature selection.


Below are two real predictors of diabetes progression, body mass index and blood pressure. Tighten the budget and watch the solution walk from the OLS point toward the origin. Under L1 it eventually hits a corner and stays glued to the axis. Under L2 it approaches the origin forever and never quite lands on it.

λ = 0.01

The pink ring is the error level the solution achieves. The dashed trail is the route the solution takes as the budget tightens. Notice the kink where the lasso path meets the axis.

Switch to the hybrid and you get the compromise that gives ElasticNet its name: a budget region that is mostly diamond but with its edges rounded by a dose of L2. It keeps the corners, so it can still zero features out, while the rounding softens the tie-breaking that makes pure Lasso erratic. We will see exactly why that matters shortly.

Watching Features Die

Two coefficients fit on a page. Real problems do not. The instrument that scales is the regularization path: plot every coefficient as the penalty sweeps from nothing to crushing, and read the model's priorities straight off the chart.


The data is the classic diabetes dataset [3]: 442 patients, ten baseline measurements, and a target measuring disease progression one year later. One preprocessing note that is not a footnote at all. A penalty on coefficient size is only fair if the features are on comparable scales, otherwise a variable measured in millimetres gets taxed a thousand times harder than the same variable measured in metres. Every feature here is standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 before anything else happens. Regularization without standardization is not regularization, it is a unit-conversion bug.

Read the lasso path left to right and it becomes a ranking. The lines that survive the longest under pressure are the variables the data insists on: body mass index and s5, a measure of blood triglycerides. The lines that collapse to zero almost immediately were never carrying much of their own weight. This is why practitioners reach for Lasso even when they do not need the regularization: the path is a feature-importance report that comes free with the model.


Now flip to ridge and watch the same ten lines behave completely differently. They glide toward zero together and asymptotically, and not one of them ever arrives. Ten features go in, ten features come out, all politely shrunk. The geometry from the previous section is right there in the shapes: no corners, no deletions.


The lower chart is where the tuning lesson lives. Training error rises monotonically as you tighten the penalty, which is unsurprising since we are handicapping the model on purpose. Held-out error does something more interesting: it falls first, bottoms out, then climbs. That valley is the only thing that matters, and it is why \(\lambda\) is never chosen by taste. In production you locate it with cross-validation (LassoCV, RidgeCV), which repeats this split many times and averages the curves.

When Lasso Cannot Choose

Lasso's decisiveness is its selling point, and also its weakness. Suppose two of your features are near-duplicates: two nearly identical lab assays, or "total floor area in square metres" alongside "total floor area in square feet". Both are genuinely predictive, and they carry the same information.


Lasso is paying by total coefficient size, so it has no incentive to split the work between two identical features when one will do. It picks a winner and zeroes the loser. The problem is that the tie is essentially arbitrary, broken by whatever noise happens to be in your particular sample. Collect the data again and Lasso may hand the credit to the other twin.


Below, two synthetic predictors correlated at 0.98, plus four pure-noise features that both models correctly delete. Each press repeats the entire experiment: a brand new sample drawn from the same process, both models refitted from scratch. Watch which twin Lasso crowns.


Over forty runs of this, Lasso handed the crown to twin A 19 times and to twin B 21 times, splitting the pair's credit 31% unevenly on average. ElasticNet, on the same forty samples, kept them within 5% of each other every time. The winner under Lasso is not a finding about the world. It is a coin flip decided by whichever twin happened to catch the noise.

This is what ElasticNet was invented to fix. The L2 component prefers to spread weight across correlated features rather than concentrate it, because squaring makes one big coefficient cost more than two medium ones. Two coefficients of 1 cost 2 under L2; one coefficient of 2 costs 4. The L1 component still deletes the genuine deadweight. You get sparsity where you want it and stability where you need it, at the cost of a second hyperparameter to tune.


A closing warning, because it is the most common way this all goes wrong in practice. Regularization is not free virtue, and there is no default value of \(\lambda\) that is safe. In the notebook this article grew out of, an untuned ElasticNet scored an \(R^2\) of 0.099 on this same diabetes data, against 0.45 for plain unregularized OLS. The penalty was strong enough to crush real signal along with the noise. A constrained model that has not been tuned is not a safer model, it is just a differently broken one.

The Verdict

Three penalties, one dial, and a decision that comes down to what you want the model to do with features it distrusts.

Penalty Reach for it when… Does to coefficients Solver Watch out for
Ridge (L2) Features are correlated and you believe most of them carry a little signal. The safe default upgrade to OLS. Shrinks all of them smoothly toward zero, never reaching it. Closed form, one term on the diagonal Keeps every feature, so it buys you no interpretability and no sparsity.
Lasso (L1) You suspect most features are irrelevant and you want the model to name the ones that are not. Sets most of them to exactly zero, shrinks the rest. Coordinate descent, soft thresholding Arbitrary tie-breaking among correlated features; cannot keep more than n features when p > n.
ElasticNet Features are both numerous and correlated in groups, and you want sparsity without the coin flips. Zeroes the deadweight, splits credit within correlated groups. Coordinate descent Two hyperparameters instead of one; needs a real search over both.
None (OLS) You have far more samples than features, low correlation, and you need unbiased coefficients for inference. Nothing. Coefficients go wherever the error surface says. Normal Equation Explodes under redundancy; impossible when p > n.

One last reframing worth carrying forward. Everything on this page has a Bayesian twin. Ridge is exactly what you get by assuming a Gaussian prior on the coefficients, and Lasso by assuming a Laplace prior, which is sharply peaked at zero. Under that reading, \(\lambda\) is not an arbitrary tuning knob at all: it encodes how strongly you believed, before seeing any data, that most effects in your problem are small. Regularization is prior knowledge, written as arithmetic.

A single mistyped value can drag a least-squares line across the room, and squaring the errors is precisely what makes it so easy. That story is called robust regression.
