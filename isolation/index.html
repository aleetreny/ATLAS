Alone

Four detectors, four different things meant by strange, and a ranking that swaps with the dataset

aleetreny

Module 2.3 · Anomaly Detection · Alone

The previous article ended on a defect rather than a method. Every classical rule for calling a point strange estimates its yardstick from the sample that contains the suspect, and a batch of eight planted bottles was enough to make itself invisible inside a hundred and seventy-eight real ones. The mean moved to them, the covariance grew towards them, and the rule that was supposed to find them was written by them.


There are four ways out on the map, and the interesting thing is that no two of them mean the same thing by strange.


An isolation forest asks how few random cuts it takes to separate a point from everything else. There is no centre in it, no distance and no density: a strange point is one that falls out early. Local outlier factor compares a point's density with the density of its own neighbours, so strange is relative to the neighbourhood rather than to the dataset, and a sparse region full of ordinary points stays ordinary. A one-class SVM draws a boundary around the data in a kernel space and asks only which side of it you are on. And the minimum covariance determinant keeps the ellipse of the previous article and repairs its estimator: fit on the tightest half of the rows, then measure everybody against that.


All four run in this page and are checked at load time against the references the generator produced.

Count The Cuts

Nobody Has Said What Normal Is

Two clusters, one tight and one loose, plus twelve points that belong to neither. The rings are the truth, and no algorithm on this page ever sees them.

One Cut, At Random

Pick one of the two measurements at random, pick a value between its smallest and its largest, and cut there. That is the entire step, and it is repeated on each half.

The Strange One Falls Out Early

Follow one of the ringed points. A point sitting on its own in an empty part of the plane gets separated quickly, because almost any cut in almost any direction puts it alone.

An Ordinary One Does Not

Follow a point in the middle of the tight cluster instead. Every cut that separates it has to separate it from a hundred neighbours first.

That Difference Is The Detector

Average the number of cuts over a forest of these trees, divide by what a random point would have cost, and you have a score.

The Forest Has Two Numbers In It

A path length is turned into a score by dividing it by what a random point in a sample of the same size would have cost, which is the average depth of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree:

$$s(x) = 2^{-\,\mathbb{E}[h(x)]\,/\,c(\psi)}, \qquad c(n) = 2H_{n-1} - \frac{2(n-1)}{n}$$

One half means exactly average. Above it means shorter to isolate than a random point, and below it means longer.

Strange Compared To Whom

A distance to the kth neighbour is the simplest anomaly score there is, and on data with two densities it has a problem it cannot fix.


Breunig's answer is to score a point against its own neighbourhood rather than against the dataset. Take the distance to the kth neighbour and call it the k-distance; smooth every distance to a neighbour upwards to at least that neighbour's own k-distance, which stops a point in a tight cluster from having an explosive density; average those, invert, and you have a local density. Then

$$\mathrm{LOF}_k(p) = \frac{1}{|N_k(p)|}\sum_{o \in N_k(p)} \frac{\mathrm{lrd}_k(o)}{\mathrm{lrd}_k(p)}$$

is the average ratio of a neighbour's density to your own. One is the neutral value, and it means "as dense as the company I keep", which is a sentence with no units in it and no reference to the rest of the data.

A Boundary, And Nothing Inside It

The third answer gives up on scoring the inside of the data at all.


Schölkopf's one-class SVM separates the data from the origin in a kernel space with the largest possible margin, which on the original axes is a boundary wrapped around wherever the points are. Its decision function is the only thing a fitted model is:

$$f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i \, K(x_i, x) - \rho$$

The parameter nu is advertised as the fraction of the data you are willing to leave outside, and that advertisement is a theorem with two halves.

nu 0.1 gamma 0.5
Gamma Settings where the outlier bound holds Settings where the support vector bound holds Worst overshoot

One half of the theorem holds everywhere and the other does not.

The Ellipse, Repaired

The fourth answer does not abandon the previous article's ellipse. It abandons the estimator behind it.


Rousseeuw's minimum covariance determinant looks for the h rows whose covariance has the smallest determinant, which is to say the tightest half of the data, and estimates the mean and the covariance from those alone. Finding that subset exactly is out of the question, and the search that makes it practical is three lines: fit on the rows you currently trust, score everybody against that fit, keep the h closest. The determinant cannot go up when you do that, so the loop cannot wander.

The Verdict

Four detectors, four definitions, and four different ways to be wrong.

Reach for When What it buys Watch out for
Isolation forest Many rows, many columns, and no idea what the data looks like Measured above. The cuts.
Local outlier factor Regions of genuinely different density inside one dataset Measured above. k, and the distance table.
One-class SVM A boundary is what you want, and clean training data to draw it from Measured above. Two parameters, and one of them decides the other.
MCD One elliptical cloud, more rows than columns, and up to half of it wrong Measured above. The shape assumption.

What the four have in common is what they gave up.


That closes anomaly detection, and with it the last question this module asks about single rows. There is one branch left on the map, and it changes the object entirely: not a point in a space with coordinates, but a basket, a set of things that happened together, with no distance between any two of them and no notion of a middle. The question stops being "is this row strange" and becomes "what goes with what", and the answer turns out to be an exhaustive search over a number of subsets nobody can write down, made feasible by one property so simple it is almost a triviality.
