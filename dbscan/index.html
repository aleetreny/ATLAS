DBSCAN

A cluster is where the points are thick: density instead of distance, "none of the above" as an answer, and nobody chooses k

aleetreny

Module 2.1 · Clustering · Density

Two articles, two defeats, one dataset. The concentric rings scored ARI -0.003 under k-means and 0.002 under the Gaussian mixture: not "hard", but impossible, because both models describe a cluster by where its middle is, and a ring's middle is the one place it never visits.


What actually makes each ring feel like a group has nothing to do with centres or shapes. Each ring is dense inside and empty around. DBSCAN is that sentence turned into an algorithm: pick a radius \(\varepsilon\) and a count minPts, call a point a core if its \(\varepsilon\)-ball holds at least minPts points, chain overlapping cores into clusters, adopt the stragglers on each chain's edge as border points, and call everything else noise.


Three things fall out of that definition that nothing earlier in this module could do. Clusters can be any shape, because chains follow the data instead of a formula. Nobody chooses k: the number of clusters is discovered, not requested. And for the first time, a point can belong to nothing, which every real dataset deserves. The price is two new dials, and this article is honest about them: the whole algorithm runs live in this page, every reference answer is checked against scikit-learn at load, and the last section is a dataset where no radius works at all, with the fix that earns HDBSCAN its H.

Cores, Borders, Noise

The two moons, the shape the density idea is usually sold on, and with reason: k-means scored 0.275 here, the mixture 0.434, and both drew straight or elliptical borders through the middle of each crescent.

The Shape That Breaks Centres

Each moon's own points sit close together; the two moons interleave so deeply that each crescent's centre of mass lies closer to the other moon's tips than to its own. Any centre-based model is doomed from the geometry alone. Forget centres. Look at spacing.

One Ball, One Question

Around every point, draw a ball of radius 0.3 and count. The solid ball sits in a crowd; the dashed one, on a thin fringe, holds barely a handful. That count is the entire local machinery: no model, no fit, one number per point.

The Cores

Every point whose ball holds at least 5 becomes a core: the crossed-out points are the ones that fail the test. Notice the cores already trace both moons. Density found the skeleton before any clustering happened.

Chain, Adopt, Reject

Cores whose balls overlap are chained into one cluster. Non-cores within reach of a chain are adopted as border points (ringed). The rest are noise, and at this tight radius 6 points get that verdict: honest strays on the thin fringes.

The Right Radius

At \(\varepsilon = 0.45\) the chains span each moon without bridging the gap between them: two clusters, ARI 0.987, from an algorithm that never once computed a centre. Everything now rides on that phrase "the right radius", which is the rest of this article.

The Two Dials

Everything DBSCAN does is decided by \(\varepsilon\) (how far "neighbouring" reaches) and minPts (how crowded a ball must be to count as dense). Below, both dials drive this page's own implementation, live, on all four of this article's datasets. Two moments to hunt for. On the rings: the redemption. Between \(\varepsilon = 0.6\) and 1.55 the score sits at a flat ARI 1.000, and below 0.6 the sparse outer ring shatters into fragments (15 of them at 0.3), because a chain is only as connected as its longest gap. On the impostors: 30 uniform background points seeded among the familiar three blobs. At \(\varepsilon = 0.8\), minPts 8, DBSCAN hands 18 of the 30 to the noise bin while keeping all three blobs whole. The other 12 impostors landed inside a blob by luck, where no algorithm, and no oracle, could tell them from citizens.

ε = 0.45 minPts = 5

Reading the Radius off the Data

The classic recipe for choosing \(\varepsilon\) is almost folklore: sort every point by the distance to its 4th neighbour, plot the curve, and put the radius at the knee, where the flat shelf of in-cluster spacing turns into the climb of emptiness. Below, the folklore becomes an instrument: the dashed line is the epsilon. Drag it and the moons re-cluster on the right in real time. What the exercise actually teaches is gentler than the recipe implies: the knee is not a point but a plateau, and any radius on the shelf works. The curve's real job is telling you where the shelf ends.

One Radius Cannot Serve Two Densities

Now the honest failure, built by search so that it genuinely bites: two tight clusters a small gap apart, and one diffuse, populous cluster far away. The radius that keeps the diffuse cluster in one piece (0.64 and up) is wider than the gap between the tight pair, which fuses from 0.48. The best any single \(\varepsilon\) manages, over an exhaustive sweep, is the compromise at 0.44: pair separated, but the diffuse cluster in six pieces with only 59% of it in the largest one, ARI 0.749.


One measurement trap to name before you trust any score here: at \(\varepsilon = 0.16\) the ARI reads a spectacular 0.973, earned by declaring the diffuse cluster entirely noise. The index rewards the coincidence that "the noise class" and "cluster three" contain the same points. Calling something nonexistent is not finding it, which is why the right panel tracks the two goals that matter instead: is the tight pair still separate, and how much of the diffuse cluster survives in one piece. Watch them never succeed together.


HDBSCAN dissolves the dilemma by refusing to fix a radius at all. Conceptually it runs DBSCAN at every \(\varepsilon\) at once, watches clusters be born, merge and die as the radius grows, and keeps the ones that stay recognisable the longest, each at its own density. One knob remains, the minimum cluster size, and it is far more forgiving: press its three values below and watch the same three clusters survive all of them.

ε = 0.44

The measured verdict: HDBSCAN at minimum size 10 delivers all three clusters, 91% of the diffuse one held together, ARI 0.934, against the 0.749 ceiling of the best possible single radius. For completeness: the Gaussian mixture also solves this particular dataset (ARI 1.0), because these clusters happen to be Gaussian blobs. Bend them into moons or rings and the mixture is gone, while density does not care. That indifference to shape, plus the refusal to fix a scale, is the pairing that makes HDBSCAN the default density tool today.

The Verdict

Density against everything the module has so far.

Decision Default Why Watch out for
Arbitrary shapes Density, not centres Measured across three articles: rings went -0.003 (k-means), 0.002 (mixture), 1.000 (DBSCAN). Chains also connect what you meant to keep apart: one bridge point can fuse two clusters.
Choosing ε The k-distance shelf, then verify On the moons, everything from 0.3 to 0.6 works: the knee is a plateau, not a needle. The plateau exists only when all clusters share a density. When they do not, no ε exists.
Outliers in the data Let noise be an answer 18 of 30 planted impostors correctly rejected here; k-means dealt every one into a cluster. Noise is a verdict about density, not truth: 12 impostors sat inside blobs and were absorbed.
Densities differ per cluster HDBSCAN The trap: best single radius 0.749, HDBSCAN 0.934, and its one knob tolerated all three tested values. It prunes sparse fringes as noise by design; expect fewer, cleaner members than you drew.
Scoring a noise-producing model Never a single index blindly ARI 0.973 at ε = 0.16 was earned by erasing a cluster. Track per-goal metrics too. Any scalar can be gamed by a degenerate answer; this article's sweep charts the goals separately.

There is a quiet admission inside HDBSCAN worth sitting with. To beat the single radius, it had to consider every radius, and at every radius the data had a different clustering: clusters inside clusters, merging as the scale grew. The flat answer it returns is a summary of a richer object, a tree of nested groupings, chosen by a rule about persistence. The next article stops summarising and builds that tree explicitly, pair by pair, for all 178 wines at once, and hands you the blade that cuts it wherever you decide the story should stop.
