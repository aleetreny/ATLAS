Calibration

When a model says 90%, it should happen nine times out of ten: repairing broken probabilities

aleetreny

Module 1.2 · Classification · Probabilities

The previous article ended with five confident liars: models whose rankings were intact but whose probabilities claimed a 4% world where reality held 1%. It promised the damage was fixable. This article is the fix, and it is the last piece of the classification story this module set out to tell, because a classifier's real output was never the label. It was always the number in front of it.


The setting is the same forest: all 2,747 cottonwood stands among 274,700 cells at a true prevalence of 1%. One new cut matters more than anything else on this page: a quarter of the training data, 41,205 cells, is held out as a calibration set. Both repairs below are themselves fitted models, and fitting them on the data they will be judged on would be the oldest sin in this atlas. So: 123,615 cells to train, 41,205 to calibrate, 109,880 to keep everyone honest.


Four patients, four different diseases. A logistic regression that tells the truth out of the box. A naive Bayes so overconfident it only ever says "certainly" in both directions. A random forest that knows the terrain better than anything else here and mumbles about it. And the SMOTE-inflated logistic carried over from last article, wrong by a near-constant factor. Two classical repairs, Platt scaling and isotonic regression, get applied to all four, with the isotonic algorithm running live in this page so you can watch it pool its way to a staircase.


And at the end, the catch that gives this article its second half: calibration can make any model honest, and honesty is not the same as knowing anything. A dummy that answers "1%" forever is almost perfectly calibrated. The difference between that dummy and a good model has a name, a number and a decomposition, and it closes the loop this module opened when the first classification article asked what a probability is worth.

What a Probability Promises

A claimed probability is a testable statement about frequency. The test is called a reliability diagram, and here is one being assembled, on the real test-set claims of the SMOTE model the last article broke.

The Claims

For every one of the 109,880 held-out cells, the model states a probability that the tree is there. Group the claims into ten bands. Nearly 104,000 cells hear "under 10%", and 2,348 hear "over 90%". Each claim is a promise about how often the tree should turn out to be real.

The Audit

For each band, plot the average claim against the measured truth: how often the tree actually stood in those cells. Ten promises, ten verdicts. The claims marched up toward certainty; the measured rate crawled along near zero until the very end.

The Standard

An honest model sits on the diagonal: cells told "55%" contain the tree 55% of the time. This model told 401 cells "about 55%" and the true rate in them was 1%. The ranking behind these numbers is nearly perfect, which is exactly what makes the lie dangerous: nothing else about the model warns you.

The Gaps

Every dashed vertical is a broken promise: claimed on one end, true on the other. Note the top one: 2,348 cells told "97%" where the measured rate is 42%. It is not even the longest gap drawn, but weighted by the crowd that heard it, it does the most damage, which is exactly where the next step goes. If a triage queue, an expected cost, or a human decision reads that number, it inherits the gap.

One Number

Weight each gap by the cells that heard it and average: the expected calibration error. This model's ECE is 0.029, which reads small until you remember the base rate is 0.01: the average claim is off by three times the entire phenomenon. The rest of this article is about driving that number to zero and what doing so cannot buy.

Four Ways to Get It Wrong

Miscalibration is not one disease. The same training data produced these four models, and each distorts its probabilities in its own characteristic shape, for its own structural reason. The shapes matter because the repair has to be able to represent the inverse of the distortion: a lesson the before-and-after section below will make concrete.


Two are worth singling out. Naive Bayes multiplies 54 correlated features as if they were independent witnesses, so its certainty compounds until only 0 and 1 remain: ECE 0.059, the worst on the page, despite a respectable AUROC of 0.970. The random forest is its opposite in every way: the best ranker this forest has ever seen in this atlas, AUROC 0.9995, exactly as the ensembles article would predict, yet its vote-share probabilities compress toward the middle from both ends. Knowing and saying are different skills.

The Two Classical Repairs

Both repairs learn a function from the model's raw score to a corrected probability, using only the calibration set. The difference is how much freedom that function gets.


Platt scaling assumes the distortion is a stretched or shifted sigmoid and fits exactly two numbers:

$$ q \;=\; \sigma\!\big(a\,z + b\big), \qquad z = \operatorname{logit}(p) $$

Two parameters cannot overfit much of anything, which is Platt's whole personality: fast, stable, and wrong whenever the distortion is not sigmoid-shaped.


Isotonic regression assumes only one thing: a higher score should never mean a lower probability. Subject to that single constraint, it finds the least-squares fit, which turns out to be a staircase, and the algorithm that builds it is a small marvel called pool adjacent violators. Sort the calibration points by score, give every point its own block, and merge any adjacent pair of blocks that violates the ordering, replacing both with their pooled average. Repeat until nothing violates. That is the entire algorithm, and it is running below on 300 real calibration cells. One deliberate twist: the score being calibrated is not a model at all. It is elevation plus distance to water, negated and rescaled, the hand-made hunch that a riparian tree lives low and wet. Any actual model from this page orders these cells almost perfectly and leaves the algorithm nothing to pool; the crude score leaves real violations, and it makes isotonic regression's quietest selling point concrete: it asks for an ordering, not a model. The draw is balanced, half tree and half not, so the merging is visible rather than pinned to the floor.

Before and After

Every patient, every treatment, measured on the untouched test set. The headline: naive Bayes goes from ECE 0.059 to 0.0001 under either repair. The SMOTE model drops from 0.029 to 0.0013 and its implied base rate returns from 3.9% to 0.99%, closing the wound the last article opened. The random forest's S-shaped mumble is the one case where the extra freedom of the staircase visibly beats Platt's fixed sigmoid shape, exactly as the shapes predicted.


Watch two things that do not move. AUROC survives every repair, because both corrections are monotone: relabelling the rungs of a ladder reorders nothing (isotonic's flat treads can tie a few cells together and shave a fraction of a point). And the logistic baseline is immune: already on the diagonal, both repairs return it essentially unchanged, which is the sound a well-specified model makes.


The SMOTE patient has one extra button. When the distortion is a known prior shift, trained at 50%, deployed at 1%, the exact undo is arithmetic: subtract the log odds of the distortion from every logit. No calibration data at all, and it lands within a whisker of the fitted repairs. Breaking a model in a way you can write down is half of already having fixed it.

How Much Data Does Honesty Cost

Holding out 41,205 cells was a luxury. The real question in practice is how small the calibration set can get before the repair itself becomes the unreliable part. So: repair the SMOTE logistic, the patient with the most to gain, on ever-smaller slices, eleven seeded draws per size, and measure the test ECE each time. The shape of this curve is the practical answer to "which method, and how much data should I reserve for it".

Calibrated Is Not the Same as Useful

Now the catch. The dummy that answers "1%" for every cell has an ECE of 0.0000: it is the most honest model on this page, and it knows nothing at all. Calibration is a promise about frequencies, not a measure of knowledge, and a score exists that holds both at once. The Brier score decomposes, bin by bin, into

$$ \text{Brier} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{reliability}}_{\text{lies, penalised}} \;-\; \underbrace{\text{resolution}}_{\text{knowledge, rewarded}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{uncertainty}}_{\text{the world's own noise}} $$

Uncertainty is the base rate's variance, 0.0099 here: the floor nobody escapes. Reliability is the calibration failure this article has been repairing. Resolution measures how far the model's bins pull away from the base rate: how much sorting of the world it actually does. The repairs drove every reliability penalty toward zero. They moved resolution barely at all, because no relabelling of claims can create distinctions the model never made. Honest and sharp are bought in different shops: sharpness in training, honesty in calibration, and a model worth deploying needs both receipts.

The Verdict

The whole article as the decisions it leaves you with.

Situation Do this Why Watch out for
Anything reads your probabilities Plot the reliability diagram before shipping AUROC hid every disease on this page: 0.970 and 0.9995 both looked fine while ECE ran from 0.001 to 0.059. ECE alone can hide a two-bin extremist; look at the picture, not just the number.
Choosing the repair Platt when calibration data is scarce, isotonic when it is not Two parameters against a free staircase: on the forest's S-shaped distortion isotonic won, 0.0002 against 0.0004 of ECE. Isotonic's treads tie scores together and can shave a little AUROC; Platt never does.
You resampled for imbalance Recalibrate, or undo the prior shift analytically The SMOTE model went from claiming 3.9% worlds to 0.98%; the pure-arithmetic logit shift got over 90% of the benefit with zero data. The shift only works when you know the resampling ratio; SMOTE-with-cleaning variants blur it.
Where the repair is fitted A held-out calibration split, never train or test The repair is a model. Fitted on training data it inherits the optimism; fitted on test data it is a leak. The split costs training data: here a quarter, and the models survived it fine.
Judging the result Demand honesty and sharpness together Repaired naive Bayes is honest (ECE 0.0001) and nearly useless (resolution 0.0014 against the forest's 0.0082). A perfectly calibrated model can still be the dummy. Calibration is necessary, never sufficient.
