Hierarchical Clustering

Every clustering at once: 177 merges, one tree, and a blade you drag to decide where the story stops

aleetreny

Module 2.1 · Clustering · Hierarchies

The previous article ended on a confession: to beat a single radius, HDBSCAN secretly consulted a richer object, a tree of clusters nested inside clusters, and then flattened it before showing it to you. This article refuses to flatten. The tree is the answer.


The recipe, agglomerative clustering, is the simplest in the whole module. Start with every point as its own cluster. Find the two closest clusters. Merge them. Repeat until one remains. That is all: no k, no radius, no initialisation, no lottery, and the 177 merges it takes to fuse 178 wines record themselves as a dendrogram, the tree of the entire history. Cut the tree low and you have many clusters; cut it high, a few; and every possible cut was computed before you asked.


The single phrase hiding all the trouble is "the two closest clusters". Between points, distance is distance. Between groups, closeness is a choice, called the linkage, and this article measures four of them on the same wines, including the one that fails so famously the failure has a name. All of it, the merging, the trees, the cuts, runs in this page's own Lance-Williams implementation, checked at load time against scipy's answers on all 178 bottles.

Watch It Merge

Twenty-four points, three visible clumps, and the algorithm doing the only thing it knows how to do, while the tree writes itself on the right.

Everyone Starts Alone

Twenty-four clusters of size one. No parameters have been chosen, because there are none to choose: the algorithm needs only a way to measure distance, and it already has one.

The First Merge

The two closest points in the whole dataset fuse, and the dendrogram records the event: a bracket joining two leaves, at a height equal to the distance the merge cost. Height is the tree's unit of meaning. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Half Way

Twelve merges in, the clumps are assembling themselves from the inside out: cheap short merges first, always. Notice the tree is still short and flat. The expensive decisions have not happened yet.

Three Clusters, If You Stop Here

Twenty-one merges in, exactly three clusters remain, and they are the three clumps. Nobody asked for three. It is simply what the data holds at this height, and stopping here is nothing deeper than cutting the tree at this storey.

One Cluster, and a History

The last merges are long and expensive: the tree's tallest storeys, fusing things that were genuinely far apart. The final object is not a clustering. It is every clustering this data admits, stacked, with the cost of each fusion recorded as height. Now for a tree worth cutting.

The Blade

All 178 wines, all 13 standardised dimensions, all 177 merges: the complete ward tree, and the horizontal line across it is yours to drag. Wherever you leave it, the branches below become the clusters, the projection underneath recolours, and the adjusted Rand index against the cultivar labels (which no tree ever saw) is computed live. Cut through the tall storey and ward's k = 3 scores 0.790: a step behind k-means' 0.897 and the mixture's 0.915 on these same bottles, because a merge, once made, is forever, and greed occasionally regrets. What the fee buys is the whole hierarchy: every k at once, and the tree's tall gaps saying which splits are load-bearing.


Then press single and meet the comb. Single linkage calls two clusters as close as their two closest members, so one giant cluster grows by accreting its nearest neighbour, one bottle at a time, and no cut anywhere in that tree beats an ARI of -0.005. Average linkage hides a subtler vice: its early cuts just peel off lone outliers (ARI -0.005 at k = 3), and only by k = 8 has it shed enough strays to reach 0.793. Same distances, same wines, four different trees: the linkage is not a detail, it is the model.

What "Close" Means Between Groups

The comb deserves a controlled demonstration. Two clean blobs of 55 points, and twelve stepping stones laid between them, each about 0.4 from the next. Ask for two clusters. Single linkage, which only ever asks "who is nearest", walks the bridge like a corridor and fuses the blobs; the split it offers instead is one stray point against everything else. Ward, which charges every merge by the variance it adds, finds absorbing a chain four units long into a compact blob exorbitant, and refuses: the blobs stay whole and the bridge is split between them. Twelve points, two opposite verdicts, and the only thing that changed is the definition of "close".

When the Tree Stops Fitting

The tree's honesty has a cost sheet: agglomeration wants every pairwise distance, and the measured bill below curves upward fast, 8.2 seconds already at twenty thousand points and hopeless well before a million. BIRCH is the classic escape: stream the data once into a compact tree of running summaries (a count, a sum and a sum of squares per node: enough to know each subcluster's centre and radius), then hand the few hundred summaries, not the points, to any clusterer you like. On five 10-dimensional blobs it matches the full tree's answer exactly, ARI 1.000 at every size, and clusters 200,000 points in 2.3 seconds, a size where the full tree is simply out of reach. One honest asterisk: at two thousand points the full tree is still faster, because BIRCH's machinery has a fixed cost. Summarising only pays once there is something worth summarising.

The Verdict

The tree against the module, and the tree against itself.

Decision Default Why Watch out for
Linkage Ward, unless clusters are non-compact Measured on the wine: ward 0.790 at k = 3, complete 0.577, average -0.005, single -0.007. Ward assumes euclidean distance and compact clusters: it is k-means' worldview, inherited.
Choosing the cut Cut where the storeys are tall A tall gap means the next merge was expensive: the data resisted it. The wine tree's three-branch storey is unmistakable. A comb has no storeys. If no gap looks special, the tree is telling you the linkage failed.
Single linkage Only when chains ARE the structure Twelve stepping stones defeated 110 points on the bridge. Its nearest-neighbour logic follows filaments. On anything blob-like it builds the comb, and no cut of a comb means anything.
Flat answer needed k-means or the mixture, once k is known The tree costs accuracy for history: 0.790 against 0.897 and 0.915 on the same wines. Use the tree to DISCOVER the k and the structure; refit flat if the single partition is the product.
Data too big for a tree BIRCH first, cluster the summaries 200,000 points in 2.3 seconds at ARI 1.000, via 1,453 summaries, where the full tree cannot run. The summaries are spheres. Non-compact structure needs the density tools of the previous article.

This closes a four-article arc through clustering: hard spheres, soft bells, connected density, and now the full nested history. Four answers to "what is a cluster", each breaking exactly where its definition runs out, and every break measured on the same shared datasets. The branch map still holds unlit chips, K-Medoids, OPTICS and Mean Shift, Affinity Propagation, Spectral Clustering, and the atlas will return for them. But the deeper pull is sideways: this whole module has assumed the structure worth finding is groups. Sometimes it is directions, the few combinations of features along which the data actually varies, and finding those, with principal components and their descendants, is module 2.2's opening move.
