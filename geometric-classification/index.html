Geometric Classification

Support vector machines and discriminant analysis: where to put the wall, and what it costs to bend it

aleetreny

Module 1.2 · Classification

The previous article answered "which one?" with probability: a sigmoid, a likelihood, a number between zero and one you could hold the model to. This one throws all of that away.


Geometric classifiers treat the data as what it literally is, points with coordinates, and ask a question about space rather than about chance: where do I put the wall? Left of it, benign. Right of it, malignant. No percentages, no distributions, no likelihood to maximise. It sounds cruder than the probabilistic route and for twenty years it was the thing to beat, because the question turns out to have a beautiful answer.


Two families live here, and they are opposites. The support vector machine ignores the bulk of the data entirely and builds its wall from the handful of points nearest the frontier. Discriminant analysis ignores the frontier and models the shape of each class, letting the boundary fall out of where the two shapes tie. One is all boundary and no distribution, the other all distribution and no boundary.


Both come with a knob that buys flexibility, and both will let you buy far more than you can afford. The last third of this article is about the price, including a measurement most tutorials leave out: on the thirty real features from the previous article, the celebrated kernel trick is worth 0.36 accuracy points over a plain linear model, which is less than the noise between folds.

The Widest Street

Eighty points, two classes, and a gap you can see. The question is not whether a line separates them. Infinitely many do. The question is which one, and answering it turns out to say something about what generalisation even means.

Infinitely Many Answers

Here are five lines. Every one of them separates these eighty points perfectly, so every one scores 100% on the data you have. Nothing in "minimise the training error" prefers any of them, which means the criterion every article before this one has used has just run out of opinions.

Ask About the Empty Space

Vapnik's answer: prefer the line with the most room around it. Widen each boundary into a street until it touches a point on either side, and pick the boundary whose street is widest. It is a claim about the data you have not seen yet, made using only the shape of the data you have.

The Points That Hold It Up

Notice which points touch the kerb. On this dataset there are two, out of eighty. Delete every other point and the boundary does not move a millimetre. Those two are the support vectors, and the model is nothing but them: an SVM is a summary of its own edge cases.

One Point Ruins Everything

Now add a single observation, out in the gap where it does not belong. The data is still perfectly separable, so a hard margin still exists. It is 153 times narrower. A boundary threaded through a slot that tight is a boundary held hostage by one point, and it is the same lesson as the outlier in robust regression, in a new costume.

Buy the Width Back

So allow violations, and charge for them. The parameter C is the price of letting a point sit inside the street or on the wrong side of it. Lower the price and the model happily pays it in exchange for a wider street: from 0.20 to 1.87 units of half-width across this sweep, and every setting on it scores exactly the same 80 out of 81 on the training data.

What Actually Changed

As the street widens it swallows more points, and every point inside it becomes a support vector: four at C = 10, forty-four at C = 0.01. That is the trade in one sentence. A hard margin listens to almost nobody. A soft margin listens to half the room, and is much harder to knock over.

Writing "Widest" Down

A boundary is the set of points where \(w^{\top}x + b = 0\). Scale \(w\) and \(b\) together and the boundary does not move, so we can fix the scale by demanding that the closest points on each side sit exactly at \(\pm 1\). With that convention the street has half-width \(1/\lVert w \rVert\), and "widest street" becomes something an optimiser can chew on:

$$ \min_{w,b} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i\,(w^{\top}x_i + b) \ge 1 \;\; \forall i $$

Minimising the length of \(w\) is maximising the width of the street. That is the hard margin, and the constraint has no give in it: one point on the wrong side and there is no solution at all. The soft margin buys give by letting each point pay a fee \(\xi_i\) to break the rule:

$$ \min_{w,b,\xi} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i\,(w^{\top}x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i, \;\; \xi_i \ge 0 $$


That second form should look familiar. It is a fit term plus a penalty on the size of the coefficients, which is exactly the shape of ridge regression, with the roles of the two terms swapped: here \(C\) multiplies the errors instead of \(\lambda\) multiplying the weights, so large C is small \(\lambda\). Same trade, mirrored notation, and the sign of that mirror is responsible for a great deal of confusion.


Drag \(C\) across five orders of magnitude and watch the street breathe.

C = 1

When No Wall Will Do

Everything so far assumes a straight boundary is available. Here is a dataset where it is not: two concentric rings, one class inside the other. Every straight line through this picture gets roughly half of it wrong, and a linear SVM cannot get past 0.609 on these 220 points even when it is scored on the very data it was fitted to.


The fix is one of the genuinely great ideas in machine learning, and it is easier than its reputation. The rings are inseparable in the plane. Add a third coordinate, the squared distance from the centre, and lift each point to that height. The inner ring rises a little. The outer ring rises a lot. In three dimensions they are two flat discs at different altitudes, and now a plane, a completely ordinary flat wall, slips between them.

$$ \phi(x_1, x_2) = (x_1,\; x_2,\; x_1^2 + x_2^2) $$


Drag the lift, and press the button to keep the boundary on screen at both ends of it. Down in the plane it is a circle. Up in the lifted space it is a straight line. It is the same boundary either way, and that is the whole trick: nothing about the data changed, only the space it was measured in.

lift = 0.00

The part that makes it practical is the part you cannot see, and it is worth a paragraph. An SVM's solution depends on the training points only through dot products between pairs of them. So if all you need are the dot products in the lifted space, you never have to compute the lifted coordinates at all: you just need a function that returns the answer.

$$ K(x, z) \;=\; \phi(x)^{\top}\phi(z) \qquad \text{without ever evaluating } \phi $$

That is the kernel trick, and its most useful instance is the RBF kernel, \(K(x,z) = e^{-\gamma\lVert x - z\rVert^2}\), whose implied \(\phi\) maps into a space of infinite dimension. You could never write those coordinates down. You never need to.


What you do need is a feel for \(\gamma\), because it is the most dangerous knob in this article. It sets how far a single training point's influence reaches. Small \(\gamma\), and every point feels every other one: the boundary is smooth and the model is nearly linear. Large \(\gamma\), and each point defends only its own doorstep: the boundary shatters into an archipelago of little islands around individual observations, training accuracy hits 1.000, and the model has learned nothing.

The middle setting is what you want and the two on either side are the two ways to lose, so press all three. Note that the failure at \(\gamma = 100\) is invisible from the training set: the model is perfect on the data it was shown and gets one in five held-out points wrong. Nothing about the fit complains. Only a test set tells you, which is the whole reason there is always a test set.

Boxes Instead of Walls

The other family in this article starts from the opposite end. Instead of hunting for a frontier, assume each class is a Gaussian cloud, estimate the clouds, and let the boundary be wherever the two clouds are equally dense. Every reader of the previous article has already met this idea: it is naive Bayes with the independence assumption removed.


The single decision that splits this family in two is whether the classes are allowed to have different shapes.


LDA insists they share one covariance matrix: the clouds may sit in different places but must have the same spread and the same tilt. Under that assumption the quadratic terms in the two densities are identical, they cancel when you take the ratio, and the boundary that survives is exactly linear. QDA gives each class its own covariance, nothing cancels, and the boundary is a conic: an ellipse, a parabola, a hyperbola, whatever the two shapes imply.


More freedom is not free, and here the bill is easy to compute. A covariance matrix over \(d\) features holds \(d(d+1)/2\) distinct numbers. LDA estimates one of them from the whole dataset; QDA estimates one per class from that class alone. On thirteen features that is 91 numbers per class, and below the chart's first few sample sizes QDA does not merely do badly: it cannot be fitted at all, because the covariance it needs to invert is singular.

Three Ways to Choose an Axis

LDA has a second life that has nothing to do with classifying. Finding the direction that separates the classes best is a projection problem, and with \(k\) classes it yields \(k-1\) useful axes for free. That makes it the supervised sibling of a technique from the very first article.


These are the same 178 Italian wines from Classical Regression, thirteen chemical measurements each, projected onto two axes three different ways. PCA asks only "where is the variance?" and never sees a label. PLS asks "what covaries with the target?", which sounds right until you notice we handed it a class index and it is built for a continuous one. LDA asks the question the picture is actually about: which plane pushes the class means apart while keeping each class tight?

A logistic model given nothing but those two LDA coordinates classifies all 178 wines correctly under five-fold cross validation. Given the two PCA coordinates it manages 0.961, and given PLS, 0.938. Thirteen dimensions to two, with no loss at all, because the two dimensions that were kept were chosen by asking the right question.


The PLS result deserves its own sentence, because it is the one that looks wrong. PLS is supervised, so it ought to beat PCA. It does not here, and the reason is a mismatch nobody warns you about: PLS maximises covariance with a numeric target, and "cultivar 0, 1, 2" is not a numeric target. It is three names that happen to be written as digits. Feed an ordinal encoding to a method that assumes an ordering and it will cheerfully optimise for a fiction.

Was the Kernel Worth It?

The rings needed the kernel. Reality mostly does not, and this is the part of the SVM story that gets skipped between the beautiful geometry and the infinite-dimensional feature space.


Here are six models on the 569 breast biopsies from the previous article: thirty real features, no synthetic structure, five-fold cross validation with the spread drawn as a whisker so nobody has to trust a single split. Three of the models are constrained to a flat boundary. Three are not.

The best kernel model beats the best linear model by 0.36 accuracy points, and the fold-to-fold standard deviation of both is larger than that. On this data, the honest report is that the kernel bought nothing you could measure. It is not that RBF kernels do not work. It is that thirty carefully engineered medical features have already done the lifting that \(\phi\) was going to do, and a boundary that was going to be nearly flat anyway does not get better for being allowed to curve.


There is a second cost that this dataset is too small to show. Kernel SVMs need the pairwise kernel matrix, which is \(n \times n\): at a thousand rows that is a million entries and nobody notices, at a million rows it is a trillion and the method is simply unavailable. Linear SVMs and logistic regression scale with \(n\), not \(n^2\). That asymmetry, more than any accuracy table, is why the kernel SVM's decade as the default classifier ended when datasets grew.


And one thing an SVM will never give you, which the previous article spent three thousand words arguing for: a probability. The decision function is a signed distance from a wall, not a likelihood, and squashing it through a sigmoid after the fact (Platt scaling) is a patch fitted on held-out data, not something the model knew.

The Verdict

Four geometries, and the choice between them is mostly a question about how many rows you have and how much you trust your features.

Model Reach for it when… Boundary It assumes… Watch out for
Linear SVM Wide data, many features, and you want the boundary decided by the hard cases. A flat wall, as far from both classes as it can get. Nothing about the distributions. Only that a flat wall is enough. C is a regularisation strength in disguise, and it runs the opposite way to λ.
RBF SVM Genuinely curved structure, a few thousand rows at most, and time to tune two knobs. Anything, up to and including nonsense. That similarity falls off with distance at a rate you have to pick. γ too high looks perfect in training and fails silently. Memory grows as n².
LDA Few rows per class, or you want k−1 supervised axes to plot. Linear, and it falls out rather than being searched for. Gaussian classes sharing one covariance. The shared-covariance assumption is usually false; it survives it better than it should.
QDA Classes with visibly different spreads, and roughly 30+ rows per class per handful of features. A conic: ellipse, parabola or hyperbola. Gaussian classes, each with its own covariance. d(d+1)/2 parameters per class. Below the crossover it is worse than LDA, then unfittable.

Stepping back across both articles of this module: we have answered "which one?" with a probability, and then with a wall. What we have not done is ask a question. Every model so far consumes all of its features at once and produces an answer in a single shot.
