ATLAS

Algorithmic Techniques for Learning And Statistics

Visual, interactive explanations of the algorithms that turn data into decisions, from drawing straight lines through point clouds to modern generative models. Every article builds the method from scratch, breaks it on purpose, and tells you exactly when to reach for it.

Module 1

Supervised Inference

You have the questions (inputs) and the correct answers (labels). The goal: learn a mapping so accurate it predicts answers for questions it has never seen: from pricing a house to diagnosing a disease.

Classical Regression

Straight lines, bent polynomials, link functions and latent projections: the four pillars of classical statistics that still power modern prediction, built from scratch and interactively.

Regularization

Ridge, Lasso and ElasticNet: why taxing a model for its own complexity makes it smarter, and why a diamond produces exact zeros where a circle never can.

Robust Regression

Huber, Theil-Sen and RANSAC: why squaring the errors hands unlimited power to a single point, and why the dangerous outliers are not the ones you can see.

Non-Parametric

Neighbours, kernels and Gaussian processes: what happens when you stop assuming an equation, and why that freedom collapses the moment you add features.

SVR and Kernel Ridge

The tube that ignores small errors and the ridge gone nonlinear: kernel ridge refits live under your fingers, and the Gaussian process turns out to be hiding inside it.

Gradient Boosting

A committee of deliberately weak trees, each fitted to the last one's mistakes, and why it still beats neural networks on the data that fills most spreadsheets.

Deep Learning for Tables

TabNet, neural additive models, and an honest measurement of why the architecture that took images and language still loses to a pile of trees on a spreadsheet.

Probabilistic Classification

Logistic regression and naive Bayes: why the sigmoid is anything but arbitrary, and why two classifiers that agree on the labels can be a factor of ten apart on how sure they are.

Geometric Classification

Support vector machines and discriminant analysis: the widest street between two crowds, the kernel that separates the inseparable, and what that kernel is really worth.

Kernel Discriminants

Turn the projection dial yourself and hit the measured linear ceiling, then give Fisher's discriminant the kernel upgrade that breaks straight through it.

Trees and Ensembles

Decision trees, bagging, random forests and extra trees: why one tree is a bad model, and the one equation that says how many you need and where adding more stops helping.

Imbalanced Learning

Hunting the one cell in a hundred: the accuracy trap, precision against recall, what SMOTE actually draws, and a measured benchmark of what resampling buys and what it silently breaks.

Calibration

A 90% that happens half the time is a lie. Reliability diagrams, Platt scaling and isotonic regression running live: repairing broken probabilities, and the thing repair can never buy.

Features Before Learning

How vision worked when a person designed the representation: HOG rebuilt live from pixels, and the invariance of SIFT and ORB measured against a ground truth instead of asserted.

Viola-Jones

Ten thousand windows per photograph, answered in milliseconds: the integral image under your mouse, the features boosting picks, and a real cascade running live in your browser.

Convolutional Networks

Type your own nine weights and watch them repaint a photograph, meet the six filters a network invented for itself, then slide the digits until both networks fall over.

Depth

Stack small kernels and watch the window grow, then watch fifty-six layers fit their own training data worse than fourteen, rule out the obvious culprit, and add the one line that fixes it.

After Depth

Seven ways to spend the same tripled budget, eleven modernisations applied one at a time, and a noise floor drawn on the ablation table so you can see how many of the rows are real.

Where

Drag two boxes until the overlap breaks, price the bill for sliding a classifier everywhere, then run a real detector in your browser and watch one unlearned constant outweigh the architecture.

Which Pixels

Find out what a perfect box is worth as a mask, watch skip connections pay for themselves only at the edges, then point at a garment the network was never taught and watch it answer anyway.

Where You Weren't

Take one ray of one photograph apart, put a number on what Fourier features are worth against a control that spends the same weights on width, then drag the camera somewhere nobody stood while a radiance field and a pile of gaussians render it side by side in your browser.

Module 2

Unsupervised Structure Discovery

Piles of evidence, no answers. Discover hidden groups, simplify complex data into something human-readable, and find the signal in the noise.

K-Means

Clustering without a teacher: Lloyd's loop running live, the initialisation lottery that k-means++ fixes, choosing k when nobody tells you, and the three shapes of data that break it.

Gaussian Mixtures

Clusters with soft edges: expectation-maximisation live under your mouse, a slider that freezes the mixture into k-means, and the failure gallery redeemed twice out of three.

DBSCAN

A cluster is where the points are thick: density under two live dials, the rings finally solved, "belongs to nothing" as an answer, and the trap that earns HDBSCAN its H.

Hierarchical Clustering

All 178 wines as one tree and a blade you drag: agglomerative clustering built pair by pair, four linkages with one famous disaster, and BIRCH for when the tree stops fitting.

Assumptions

K-medoids, k-modes, OPTICS and mean shift: push one outlier away until the mean gives up, renumber the categories and watch one method not care, and cut the plot that holds every radius at once.

Affinities

Affinity propagation and spectral clustering never look at a point, only at the n² numbers between them. Watch messages elect exemplars with nobody saying k, bend the space until two rings come apart, and score every one of 32,767 possible cuts.

Directions

PCA, ICA and NMF are one factorisation under three constraints. Drag a direction and watch variance trade against error, turn one dial until two mixed signals come apart, then rebuild a digit out of atoms that may cancel or may not.

Distances

Delete the columns, keep only how far apart the wines are, and the same map comes back. Then bend the numbers until only their order survives, and split a variance into the part that is shared and the part that is nobody else's business.

Unrolling

Two points a hand's width apart in the room can be a corridor apart along the wall. Build the neighbourhood graph yourself, find the few edges that ruin it, and watch a grid fit the data perfectly while recovering nothing.

Neighbours

Stop reproducing distances, keep only who is next to whom, and a plane has room. Run the gradient descent yourself, then watch a clump counter collapse to one group while the data never changes, and measure what happened to the sizes and the gaps.

Bottleneck

Every method so far solved for its coordinates and left nothing behind. Fit two functions instead: drag a crosshair anywhere in the plane and a decoder answers, including where no digit ever was.

Odd One Out

Three classical rules for calling a reading strange, and the defect they share: the yardstick is estimated from the data that contains the suspect. Push a bottle out and watch the band grow to swallow it.

Alone

Four detectors that mean four different things by strange: cuts that isolate, neighbours that compare, a boundary that encloses, and half the data thrown away. No two of them agree on which point is odd.

Bought Together

Change the object from a point to a basket and distance, averages and directions all disappear. What is left is counting, over a number of subsets with fifty-one digits in it.

Module 3

Generative AI

Learn the underlying distribution of reality to create new samples that never existed. The shift from "classifying a cat" to "drawing a cat".

Counterfeit

Two networks that want opposite things. Watch four divergences pick four different answers out of the same family, see the gradient of the original loss die where the model is worst, and train a GAN in your own tab until it forgets half the data.

Style

A latent space nobody asked anything of, and what changes when you do. On sprites whose factors are known exactly and whose joint distribution has a hole in it: mix two samples layer by layer and measure which idea each block was holding.

Translation

One picture into another, with the answer in hand and then without it. Why an averaging loss is obliged to blur, how wide a patch critic really sees, and what a cycle constraint fails to pin down. Draw a plan and both translators answer live.

Somewhere to Sample From

The last article left a decoder that answers anywhere and no reason to believe a picked point is somewhere it answers well. Put a distribution there on purpose, then check the bound against a log likelihood computed exactly.

A Finite Alphabet

Make the code a symbol from a fixed list and the rate stops being an estimate and becomes a count of bits. Turn one cell of a four entry code by hand, and meet the gradient the encoder needs, measured at exactly zero.

The Constant Nobody Computes

Any function becomes a density once you divide by its own integral. Run the chains yourself, take the noise out and watch them stop sampling, and read the bias of the usual shortcut against a gradient that is not one.

Exactly

A change of variables is an identity, not a bound, so this model hands back the log likelihood itself. Warp the plane one layer at a time, and then watch a smooth invertible map fail to open a hole it is not allowed to tear.

How Many Bits Is a Picture

An exact likelihood on images is a compression rate, so it can be put beside gzip and PNG on the same bytes. Sample it in your browser, invert a real digit exactly, and ask which of two datasets it thinks is more likely.

A Thousand Easy Problems

Destroy the data with noise whose every step is a gaussian, then learn to take one step back. Here the forward process is closed form, so you can see the exact score field the network is trying to learn, drawn beside the one it actually learned.

The Ceiling You Cannot See

Run the same diffusion in an autoencoder's small space and it costs a fortieth of the network. The price is a limit fixed before any diffusion is trained, measurable in one forward pass, and almost never reported.

Do As You Are Told

Tell the model what to make, then turn the dial that makes it obey harder. Obedience and realism peak at different settings, and asking for a digit it was never trained on gets you less than guessing.

Module 4

Sequential Intelligence

Data that flows: what happened before dictates what happens next. Understanding human language and forecasting the future from history.

What A Word Is Worth

Turning a document into a vector is three separate decisions, not one formula. All twelve combinations measured on one split, with a noise floor drawn in, and the decision that is not in the name turns out to be the one doing the work.

What It Is About

Thousands of axes reduced to eight, on a corpus where the eight real answers are sitting right there to check against. The collapsed Gibbs sampler runs in the page, from a random start.

The Company It Keeps

Give a word a position instead of an axis and the position has to be learned from its neighbours. Three ways of doing it on one tagged corpus, with a part-of-speech tag on every neighbour.

What Came Before

Four models of memory on one task where every token has a known right answer, and two measurements of how far back each of them can really see.

Everything At Once

Make every pair of positions one step apart and pay for it in a currency that grows with the square of the length. The trained encoder runs in the page, so the constants can be switched off.

Three Ways To Read

One architecture and one budget, asked to predict three different things. The fifteen percent masking rate is swept instead of assumed, and the column nobody prints explains the rest.

Back To Recurrence

A linear recurrence is also a convolution, exactly, so it trains in parallel and runs one step at a time. The page computes both and reports how far apart they are.

One Step Ahead

Put the rows in order and almost every habit has to be relearned. Watch two stationarity tests pass a series that is obviously seasonal, check an exact identity between a smoother and an ARIMA in your browser, and count how often an interval keeps its promise.

The Size of a Surprise

On twenty years of daily returns the next value is a coin flip and its spread is not. Fit a variance nobody ever observes with three sliders, score it against the options market, and count the days the loss went past the line.

A Forecast Is a Table

Write the past into columns and any regressor can forecast, which is what most people who forecast for a living now do. Then meet the split that lies, the ceiling a tree cannot forecast past, and a decomposition scored against a truth written down first.

Many Series at Once

One network across hundreds of series instead of one model each. Watch a stack of blocks subtract its own explanation, score its interpretable half against components written down in advance, and put a variable selection weight beside what breaking that variable costs.

A Model That Never Saw Your Data

Turn numbers into letters and train a language model on a pile of series, then ask it to finish one it has never read. Measure the floor the alphabet puts under it, and remove a whole family from the pile to find out what having seen the shape was worth.

What a Sound Looks Like

Nothing eats audio directly: it becomes a picture first. Slide the window length over a sweep whose frequency is known at every sample, hear what a spectrogram threw away, and find out what a cepstral coefficient is actually for.

One Sample at a Time

How far back a stack of dilated convolutions can see is arithmetic, not a hyperparameter, and the prediction it makes about pitch is tested here. Then listen to a model fed its own output, and watch a decoder refuse to stop talking.

Module 7

Scientific Intelligence

From correlation to causation, from confidence to guarantees, from black boxes to explanations. Asking "why?" and "what if?".

The Arrow You Cannot See

Every group in the table goes one way and the table goes the other. Watch the reversal appear as a third variable is switched on, then try to tell two worlds apart using only the data they produce.

One Number Instead of Twelve

A theorem says one number can stand in for every covariate at once. Seven estimators are run against a known answer, a deliberately wrong score is used to show what balance actually proves, and the overlap is trimmed until the question changes.

A Lever With No Other Reach

When the confounder cannot be measured at all, a good instrument still recovers the effect. Here is what makes one good, what a weak one costs, and why an assumption no data can test can be checked anyway by finding every world that fits.

Which Boxes to Tick

Adjusting for everything is the most common way to get a causal answer wrong. A graph decides which sets are valid, the rules are applied by hand to a case with no valid set at all, and then a forest is asked who the effect is largest for.

Ninety Per Cent, Guaranteed

One sorted list of residuals turns any model into intervals that cover what they promise, with no assumption about the model at all. The guarantee is exact, it is checked here against its own distribution, and it is weaker than it sounds.

The Posterior You Can Actually Draw

A network small enough that its exact posterior can be computed by brute force, so every approximation people use in practice can be marked against the right answer instead of against each other.

Credit, Exactly

Ten columns, every one of the coalitions enumerated, and a model whose true attributions are known in closed form. So the picture everybody publishes can be checked against the answer rather than admired, and the two things called SHAP can be told apart.

Where It Looked

Saliency maps are almost always shown and almost never scored. This page has the exact mask of every object, so the pictures get marked, and one of the two controls that explain nothing lands in the middle of the methods.
