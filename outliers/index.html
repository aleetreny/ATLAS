Odd One Out

Every rule for spotting a strange reading measures it with a ruler that reading helped build

aleetreny

Module 2.3 · Anomaly Detection · Odd One Out

The previous four modules asked what a point is: which class, which cluster, which two numbers stand in for its thirteen. This one asks a smaller and much older question. Is this reading the odd one, and if so, odd compared to what?


Three answers have been in every statistics textbook for decades, and they are still the first thing anybody reaches for. How many standard deviations from the mean, which is a z-score. How far past the quartiles, which is Tukey's fences and the reason a boxplot has whiskers. And, when there is more than one column, Mahalanobis distance, which is the z-score with the correlations put back in. All three are one line of arithmetic, all three are defensible, and all three share a defect that is easy to state and almost never drawn.


The yardstick is estimated from the data that contains the suspect. The mean it is measured against has already moved towards it. The spread it is divided by has already grown to accommodate it. The suspect gets a vote at its own trial, and with a second suspect to vote alongside it the two can acquit each other. None of that is a bug in an implementation; it is what the formula says.


Everything below is measured on the 178 bottles of wine that this site has been carrying since its first article, at three widths: one column, two columns, and all thirteen. The same bottles, three questions, and one answer that gets worse as the question gets wider.


Everything on this page is computed in the browser and checked at load time against the references the generator produced.

What Each Rule Calls Ordinary

Two intervals, drawn as the areas they are willing to accept. Three standard deviations either side of the mean is one of them; Tukey's fences, a step and a half of the interquartile range past each quartile, is the other. Nothing is outside either.

One Walks Out

A Ceiling With No Data In It

Put k of the n readings at some distance d and leave the rest where they are. The mean moves to kd/n, the sample standard deviation becomes

$$s = d\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n(n-1)}}$$

and the z-score of one of the k readings is the ratio of the two, in which d cancels out completely:

$$|z|_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{(n-1)(n-k)}{nk}}$$

Nothing about the data survives into that expression except how many readings there are and how many of them are odd together.

odd readings sitting together 1

The three-sigma rule has a smallest sample it can work in at all.

Two Columns Disagree With Their Own Margins

Screening a table column by column is the obvious thing to do and the wrong one.


Mahalanobis' answer is to measure distance in units the data itself supplies. Subtract the mean, then divide not by a standard deviation but by the whole covariance matrix:

$$d^2(x) = (x-\hat\mu)^{\!\top}\, \hat\Sigma^{-1} (x-\hat\mu)$$

With one column that is exactly a squared z-score. With two, the set of points at a fixed d is an ellipse tilted along whichever way the columns move together, and the rule stops being a box and starts being a shape. Both are drawn below: drag the suspect anywhere, and watch what each one says about it.

The Cutoff Everybody Uses Is The Wrong One

If the mean and the covariance were known rather than estimated, the squared distance of a gaussian point would be chi-squared on p degrees of freedom, and the textbook cutoff would be exactly right.


They are not known. They come out of the same n rows being screened, and then d² is not chi-squared at all: it is a beta distribution scaled by (n−1)²/n, which is also, and not by coincidence, the largest value it can take. Below is the false alarm rate the chi-squared cutoff actually delivers, measured by handing it gaussian samples that contain nothing to find.

The Verdict

Three rules, one shared defect, and three different amounts of it.

Reach for When What it buys Watch out for
Three sigma One column, plenty of rows, and at most one thing wrong with it Measured above. The ceiling.
Tukey's fences One column, and you would rather not assume a shape Measured above. The quartiles.
Modified z One column, and up to half of it might be wrong Measured above. Ties.
Mahalanobis Several columns that move together, and rows to spare Measured above. The estimate it is measured against.

Nothing above is an argument against these rules.


The repair has a name, and it is the same one in every case: estimate the centre and the spread with something a minority cannot drag, then measure everybody against that. The median and the MAD do it in one dimension, and the next article does it in thirteen, alongside three other detectors that give up on the idea of a centre altogether: one that looks for points that are easy to separate, one that compares each point only with its own neighbours, and one that draws a boundary and asks nothing about what is inside it. Four definitions of strange, four different answers on the same data, and a ranking that swaps when the dataset does.
