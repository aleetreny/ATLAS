Affinities

Two algorithms that never look at a point, only at the n² numbers between them

aleetreny

Module 2.1 · Clustering · Affinities

Every algorithm this module has published reads coordinates. A mean is an average of them, a covariance is measured in them, a radius is a length in them, and even a medoid, which is a point rather than an average, is chosen by adding distances up. Two algorithms are left on the map, and neither of them does.


Affinity propagation and spectral clustering both begin by throwing the points away. What they keep is the square table of how similar each pair is, n rows by n columns, and everything that happens afterwards happens on that table: messages passed along its entries in one case, the eigenvectors of it in the other. Nothing downstream can tell whether the numbers came from two dimensions, from thirteen, or from a questionnaire.


That buys the two things this module has been paying for since its first article. Nobody has to say k, and a cluster no longer has to be a blob. Both claims are true, both are measured below, and both are more expensive than they look: one of the two gets k wrong on every dataset here, and the other needs a parameter that decides what "near" means before it can start.


Both run in this page, and are checked at load time against the references the generator produced.

Watch Them Argue

Thirty points from the blobs you already know, and an algorithm with no k in it anywhere.

Everybody Applies

There is no k, so there is no shortlist. Every point is a candidate to be the centre of a cluster, and the algorithm's job is to decide which ones the others will agree on. The only input is the table of similarities: here, minus the squared distance between each pair, which is what scikit-learn hands it by default.

Responsibility: How Badly I Want You

The first message runs from a point to a candidate. r(i,k) is how much better k is for i than i's best alternative, so it is large only when the candidate beats everybody else, and negative when it does not. Drawn here from one point: thick lines are strong claims.

Availability: How Ready I Am To Serve

The second runs the other way. a(i,k) gathers the enthusiasm every other point has for k and passes it back to i as evidence that k is going to be an exemplar anyway. This is the part that makes the answer a consensus rather than a set of independent nearest-neighbour votes.

Half Of Yesterday

The two messages feed each other, so the iteration swings. Every update is therefore mixed with the previous one, and the mixing weight, the damping, is a real parameter with real consequences, measured further down. Points that have talked themselves into the job are drawn filled.

Settled

A point is an exemplar when its own two messages to itself add up to something positive, and the rest join whichever exemplar they are most similar to.

The Same Objective As Last Time

Written down, affinity propagation is maximising

$$\mathcal{S}(E)=\sum_{i \notin E} \max_{e \in E} s(i,e) \;+\; |E|\,p$$

over every possible set of exemplars E, where p is the preference, the price of opening a cluster. With s(i,e) = −‖x i − x e‖² the first term is exactly the sum the previous article minimised with k-medoids, one square heavier, and the second term is the only difference: k does not appear. It is not given; it is whatever number of exemplars pays for itself.


The two message updates are the max-sum algorithm on the factor graph of that objective, which is a sentence worth exactly one line of arithmetic:

$$r(i,k) \leftarrow s(i,k) - \max_{k' \neq k}\big[a(i,k') + s(i,k')\big]$$

$$a(i,k) \leftarrow \min\Big(0,\; r(k,k) + \sum_{i' \notin \{i,k\}} \max\big(0, r(i',k)\big)\Big)$$

Two numbers per ordered pair, updated until the set of points with r(k,k) + a(k,k) > 0 stops changing. There is no initialisation to get lucky with, no restarts, and no seed: run it twice and you get the same answer twice, which is more than k-means can say.


Whether the messages find the best set of exemplars has an answer.

The Other Way To Use A Square Table

Affinity propagation reads the table as a market: who would serve whom, and at what price. Spectral clustering reads the same table as a graph: points are vertices, similarities are edge weights, and a cluster is a piece you can cut off without cutting many edges.


Cutting few edges on its own is a bad idea, because the cheapest cut is usually one lonely point. Shi and Malik's fix is to charge the cut against how much of the graph is left on each side:

$$\text{Ncut}(A,B)=\frac{\text{cut}(A,B)}{\text{assoc}(A)}+\frac{\text{cut}(A,B)}{\text{assoc}(B)}$$

where cut is the weight crossing the boundary and assoc is all the weight touching one side. Minimising it over every possible split is NP-hard, and the way out is the whole trick: allow the indicator of the split to be a real vector instead of a yes or no, and the problem becomes an eigenvector problem in the Laplacian

$$L = I - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}$$

whose smallest eigenvectors are the relaxed answer. Take the k of them with the smallest eigenvalues, treat each point's k coordinates as its new position, scale every row to unit length, and run k-means there. That last step is not a joke at the algorithm's expense: k-means is fine once the space has been bent so that clusters are round in it, and bending the space is what the eigenvectors did.


Two facts make the spectrum worth drawing rather than just using. The number of eigenvalues equal to zero is exactly the number of connected pieces the graph is in, and the eigenvectors that go with them are those pieces' indicators. So when the graph does fall apart along the clusters, spectral clustering is not approximating anything: it is reading off the answer.

Three of those ten do not take k as an argument, which makes the column worth reading on its own.


That closes the clustering branch: six articles, twelve algorithms, one question. The pattern across them was never that the sophisticated ones win. It is that every algorithm is an assumption with a search bolted on, that the assumption is what decides which datasets it can see at all, and that the search is what you actually get. Take an assumption away and something comes back to be paid for elsewhere, and where it comes back can be measured before you commit.


What all ten have in common is the thing to leave with: they were all looking for groups. Sometimes the structure worth finding is not a grouping at all but a handful of directions, the combinations of features along which the data actually varies, and that is where module 2.2 opens, on these same 178 bottles of wine, which cluster slightly better once eleven of their thirteen dimensions have been thrown away. That module is already written end to end, and it keeps taking things away: first the coordinates, so that a table of distances is all that is left, then the distances themselves, until only who is next to whom survives, and finally the map itself, replaced by two functions that answer anywhere you point. This page's n by n table is where that road starts.
