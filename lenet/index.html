Convolutional Networks

Nine numbers dragged across a picture: how a model stopped being told what to look for and worked it out instead

aleetreny

Module 1.3 · Computer Vision · Learned Features

Everything in this branch so far has been designed by a person. HOG counts gradient directions because someone decided gradient directions were the thing to count. Viola-Jones compares rectangles because rectangles are the shapes an integral image makes free. In both cases a human chose the vocabulary and the algorithm chose only which words to use.


This article is where that stops. The operation underneath is the same one those methods already used, a small grid of weights slid across the image, but the weights are no longer written down by hand. They start as noise and are pushed around by gradient descent until they become whatever the task needed, which turns out to include an edge detector nobody asked for.


Two networks were trained for this page, on 12,000 handwritten digits and tested on 5,000 more. One is a convolutional network in the arrangement Yann LeCun published in 1998, built from modern parts; the other is an ordinary fully connected network sized so that both have almost exactly the same number of parameters. Every filter, every accuracy and every collapse below is what those two runs produced.

Nine Weights, Dragged Everywhere

Start with the operation itself, which is smaller than its reputation.

An Image Is a Grid of Numbers

A photograph, close enough that the pixels are visible squares. Each one is a single brightness value between 0 and 1. There is nothing else in there: no edges, no objects, no face, just the grid.

One Position

Put a 3 × 3 grid of weights over one small patch, multiply each weight by the pixel underneath it, add the nine results. That single number goes into the output at the position the window was centred on. That is the entire operation, written out below the panels.

Every Position

Now slide the window one pixel at a time and repeat, using the same nine weights at every stop. This is the part that matters later: the weights do not know where they are, so whatever they detect, they detect anywhere.

An Edge Map, from Nine Numbers

These particular nine weights are a centre of 4 surrounded by four −1s. Flat regions cancel to zero and come out mid grey; places where a pixel disagrees with its neighbours survive. Nobody had to define what an edge is.

Move the Input, the Output Moves

Shift the picture nine pixels and the whole edge map shifts nine pixels with it. This property has a name, equivariance, and it is the reason convolution is worth building a network out of. It is also routinely oversold, which the measurement further down settles. The bright rim along the top and left is not a glitch: filling the vacated strip with background made a real new edge there, and the filter did its job and reported it.

Choose the Nine Yourself

Before a network is allowed to pick the weights, it is worth spending a minute picking them by hand, because the famous kernels stop being incantations once you have typed one. Every cell below is editable, and the photograph on the right is recomputed on every keystroke: 25,600 output pixels, in this page, from your nine numbers.


One quantity predicts most of what you will see. Add the nine weights up. If they sum to 1, a flat region keeps its brightness and the kernel only rearranges detail. If they sum to 0, flat regions cancel exactly and only disagreement survives, which is what every edge detector in the presets has in common. The clearest way to feel it: load sharpen, whose weights sum to 1, and walk the centre cell down from 5 to 4. The sum reaches zero, and the photograph turns into its own edges without a single other cell changing.

the nine weights

Why Not Just Connect Everything?

A fully connected layer is more general than a convolution: it can learn any linear map, including every kernel above. So why constrain it? The answer is a counting argument, and the numbers are brutal.


The first layer of the network on this page produces 6 feature maps of 28 × 28, which is 4,704 output numbers from 784 input pixels. A fully connected layer of that shape needs 784 × 4,704 weights plus 4,704 biases: 3,692,640 parameters. The convolution produces the same 4,704 numbers with 6 filters of 5 × 5 plus 6 biases, which is 156. That is a factor of roughly 23,700, and it comes from two decisions:


Locality. A pixel in the top-left corner is not wired to a pixel in the bottom-right one, because in an image the informative relationships are between neighbours. Weight sharing. The detector used at the top-left is the same detector used at the bottom-right, because a stroke is a stroke wherever it lands. Neither is a law of nature; both are assumptions about images, and they are right often enough to be worth the enormous saving.


To make the saving concrete rather than theoretical, both networks here were given the same budget. The convolutional net has 61,706 parameters. The fully connected one was sized to match, with 78 hidden units and 62,020 parameters, 314 more. Same data, same optimiser, same eight epochs, same seed:

Network Parameters Test accuracy Training time
Convolutional 61,706 96.98% 4.4 s
Fully connected 62,020 93.50% 0.7 s

Three and a half points does not sound like much until you count errors instead of successes: 151 mistakes out of 5,000 against 325, which is 53.5% of the errors removed, with 314 fewer parameters to do it with. The structure is worth more than the capacity. It is also six times slower per run, which is the honest price and the reason the fully connected net was still competitive on small problems for years.

The Filters Nobody Wrote

Those 156 first-layer parameters started as random noise. Below they are after training, which is the point of the whole article: six 5 × 5 kernels, arrived at by gradient descent, with nobody having specified what any of them should detect. Click a filter or one of its maps to enlarge its weights, and switch digits along the top.


Watch the sum of the weights as you click through them, because it is the same quantity that governed the kernel editor. Exactly one of the six nearly cancels: filter 1 sums to +0.01, which makes it an edge detector in precisely the sense you were building by hand two sections ago, blind to flat regions by construction. The other five do not. They run from +0.56 up to +2.01, so they respond to how much ink is under them as well as to how it is arranged. Nobody asked for an edge detector and one appeared anyway, alongside five things that have no name.


The black regions are the ReLU, which keeps positive responses and sets every negative one to zero, so a filter reports where it agreed with what it was looking for and says nothing at all elsewhere. How much it discards is a property of the filter: on the zero the widget opens with, one map comes back 2.6% zero and another 72.8%.

These maps are not decoration. They are computed in your browser from the exported weights, by the same padded cross-correlation the training library ran, and checked at load time against feature maps recorded straight out of the trained model. The agreement is printed in the readout above.

What Sliding Actually Buys

Here is the claim you will read everywhere: convolutional networks are translation invariant, so they recognise an object wherever it appears. It is half true, and the half that is false is easy to measure. Both networks were trained on centred digits and never saw a shifted one. The slider moves the test digits diagonally, one pixel at a time.

the test digits, moved: not at all

At one pixel the convolutional net loses 2.7 points and the fully connected one loses 14.6. At two pixels it is 96.98% down to 77.00% against 93.50% down to 37.90%. So the structure genuinely helps, and the feature map in the widget shows why: it travelled with the digit instead of arriving at a different set of weights.


And then both of them fall off a cliff. By four pixels the convolutional net is at 9.76%, which is what guessing would give you, and by five and six the two curves have crossed: both sit so far below chance that neither is uncertain, they are confidently wrong, and the remaining gap says which way each one fails rather than which one still recognises a digit. Four pixels, a seventh of the width of the image, is enough to destroy a network that was supposed to be translation invariant.


The distinction the slogan loses is between equivariance and invariance. Convolution is equivariant: move the input and the feature maps move identically, as the scrolly showed. But the classifier sitting on top of those maps is a fully connected layer reading a flattened grid, and to it a stroke at position 40 and the same stroke at position 96 are different inputs entirely. Pooling buys back a pixel or two of tolerance. It does not buy invariance, and nothing in the architecture does.


What does buy it is showing the network the shifts. Retrained with random shifts of up to two pixels applied during training, and nothing else changed, the same architecture scores 49.42% on four-pixel shifts instead of 9.76%, a factor of five, while giving up nothing on centred digits (97.24% against 96.98%). The invariance was never in the model. It was in the data all along.

What Changed by 2012

LeNet worked in 1998 and then convolutional networks spent a decade as a speciality. What broke the drought in 2012 was AlexNet, and the honest summary of what it contributed is: more data, much more compute, and a short list of engineering ingredients. The scale is not something this page can reproduce, but the ingredients are, one at a time, on the same network, changing nothing else.

Ingredient Without it With it What the measurement says
ReLU instead of tanh 97.58% 96.98% Backwards. On this network tanh converged sooner and scored higher.
Dropout in the head 93.88% 95.44% Worth 1.6 points, but only once the training set is small enough to overfit.
Shifts during training 9.76% 49.42% Five times better on shifted digits, and free on clean ones.

The first row is the interesting one, because it contradicts the story everyone tells, including the story this article was going to tell. ReLU is supposed to converge faster than tanh. Here it did not: same network, same seed, same data, only the nonlinearity swapped, and tanh reached a shared training-loss target in 4 epochs against ReLU's 6, then finished ahead on the test set by 0.6 points.

That result is real and it is also narrow, and both halves matter. ReLU's advantage is a story about deep networks: a saturating activation squashes gradients towards zero at every layer it passes through, so the damage compounds with depth, and AlexNet was eight layers trained for six days on two GPUs. The network on this page is two convolutional layers deep, trained for eight epochs by Adam, which adapts its step sizes and papers over exactly the problem ReLU was solving. At this size there is nothing for ReLU to fix, so it wins nothing.


The lesson is not that ReLU is overrated. It is that an ingredient's value is a property of the regime it is used in, and quoting a benchmark result from a different regime is how a technique becomes a superstition. ReLU is kept everywhere else in this article because it is what the reference architectures use, and because the next article, where depth is the whole subject, is where its advantage should actually appear. If it does not, that measurement will get the same treatment as this one.

The Verdict

What convolution gives you, and what it does not.

Idea What it buys Measured here Watch out for
Weight sharing Detectors that work anywhere, almost free 156 parameters where a fully connected layer of the same shape needs 3,692,640. It assumes the same feature is worth looking for everywhere, which is false for aligned data like faces in passport photos.
Learned filters The vocabulary comes from the data 96.98% against 93.50% at equal parameter count, over half the errors removed. Six times slower to train here, and what the filters learn is only as good as what the data contains.
Equivariance Move the input, the features move with it A one-pixel shift costs 2.7 points instead of the 14.6 it costs a fully connected net. It is not invariance. Four pixels drops this network to what guessing would give.
Augmentation The invariance the architecture never had 49.42% against 9.76% on four-pixel shifts, at no cost on centred digits. You only get invariance to the transformations you thought to apply.

The hand-designed era ended here, but notice what did not change. Someone still chose 5 × 5 filters, six of them, two convolutional layers, max pooling of stride two. The vocabulary is learned; the shape of the network that learns it is still a human decision, and for the next decade that decision was where the whole field's effort went.


The next article in this branch takes the most obvious of those decisions, how many layers, and pushes on it until it breaks. It breaks in a way nobody expected: past a certain depth, adding layers makes the network worse on its training data, which is not overfitting and cannot be fixed by more of it. The fix is one of the shortest ideas in the field.
