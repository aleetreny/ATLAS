K-Means

Clustering without a teacher: two alternating steps, and everything that follows from them

aleetreny

Module 2.1 · Clustering

Every one of the eleven articles before this one had an answer key. A price, a diagnosis, a cover type: some column of truth to fit against, and every model was graded on how well it reproduced it. This module throws the answer key away.


Unsupervised learning gets a pile of evidence and no verdicts, and the first question it asks is the most natural one there is: do these points come in groups? Customers, cells, documents, pixels. Nobody labels them. Something still structures them.


K-means is the oldest, fastest and most-used answer, and it is two steps in a loop. Guess where the group centres are. Assign every point to its nearest centre. Move each centre to the middle of its points. Repeat until nothing moves. That loop runs live in this page, every run of it, in about thirty lines of JavaScript.


The catch is that the loop always converges and never promises to converge anywhere good. Where it lands depends on where it started, which turns a clean algorithm into a lottery, and the fix for that lottery, k-means++, is measured here across three hundred restarts. Then the two questions everyone actually hits: how do you choose \(k\) when nobody tells you, and what shapes of data quietly break the whole idea. And at the end, the honest test: the 178 wines from the first article, clustered blind, against cultivar labels the algorithm never saw.

Two Steps, Repeated

Three hundred points with three visible groups. The algorithm does not know that. It knows only distances, and the number three, which we gave it.

No Labels Anywhere

This is what the algorithm sees: coordinates, and nothing else. No colours, no answer key. Before scrolling on, decide for yourself where the three groups are, because in a moment you can check the machine against your eyes.

Guess Three Centres

Drop three centres at random data points. This is the entire initialisation of classic k-means, and it is the step everything else in this article comes back to, because these three guesses are terrible and one cluster has been given two of them.

Step One: Assign

Every point joins its nearest centre, which carves the plane into territories with straight-line borders. The colouring you see is not a model of the data. It is a map of who is closest to whom, and at this stage it is visibly wrong.

Step Two: Update

Each centre moves to the mean of the points it just claimed. The crowded centre gets pulled toward the mass it owns, the stranded one drifts toward whatever it can reach. Nothing clever happened: two averages and a move.

Loop

Assign, update, assign, update. Watch the borders sweep across the contested ground and settle. Each full pass can only ever lower the total squared distance, which is why the loop must stop: a number that only falls and cannot go below zero has nowhere to go forever.

Converged

Nothing moves any more. The final grouping agrees with the eye, and the browser's inertia matches scikit-learn's reference answer for this dataset to the decimal shown below the chart. Keep that number in mind: the next section is about runs where it comes out four times worse.

What Is Being Minimised

The loop is not arbitrary. Both steps are coordinate descent on one quantity, the inertia: the sum of squared distances from every point to its assigned centre.

$$ J \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big\lVert x_i - \mu_{c(i)} \big\rVert^2 $$

Hold the centres fixed, and the assignment that minimises \(J\) is "nearest centre", which is step one. Hold the assignments fixed, and the centre that minimises \(J\) for a group is its mean, which is step two. Each step lowers \(J\) or leaves it alone, so the loop terminates. That is the entire convergence proof, and note what it does not say: nothing about which resting place you reach. \(J\) has many local minima, and Lloyd's loop stops at whichever one is downhill from the start.


Two consequences hide in that formula and both will matter later. Squared Euclidean distance means the centre is a mean, and means chase outliers. And distance treats every direction alike, which is a quiet assumption that the groups are round. Neither is a bug. Both are choices, and the failure gallery below is what happens when the world declines them.


Run the loop yourself. One button per step, or let it run; the inertia readout only ever falls.

The Lottery of the First Guess

Press "new random start" above a few times and you will eventually see it: two centres landing in one cluster, splitting it, while two real clusters share a single centre. The loop converges, the inertia stops falling, and the answer is wrong. Converged does not mean correct.


Measured over 300 restarts on this data, purely random initialisation finds the good answer 92% of the time. The other 8% of runs land in bad basins, the worst of them at an inertia of 2,134 against the true optimum's 502: four times worse, from nothing but an unlucky first guess.


k-means++ fixes the lottery by making the guesses repel each other: the first centre is a random point, and each further centre is drawn with probability proportional to the squared distance from the nearest centre already placed. Points far from every existing centre are exactly where a missing cluster lives, so that is where the next guess tends to land. Same loop afterwards, different opening move. Across the same 300 restarts it landed in the good basin every single time.

Nobody Tells You k

We handed the algorithm the number three, which was cheating: in real unsupervised work nobody knows the number of groups, and k-means will happily produce whatever number you ask for. Ask for five and it will cut the data into five. The question is how to notice the data disagrees.


The classic tool is the elbow: fit every k, plot the inertia, and look for the bend where adding a cluster stops paying. The trouble is that inertia always falls as k grows, all the way to zero at k = n, so you are squinting for a change of slope in a curve with no peak. It works when the structure is blatant and turns into palm reading when it is not.


The silhouette asks a sharper question, per point: how close am I to my own cluster, against how close I am to the nearest other one? It rewards tight-and-separated, punishes both over-splitting and under-splitting, and therefore actually peaks. On this data it peaks at k = 3 with 0.683, and everything past 4 falls off a cliff.

k = 3

Three Ways to Break It

Everything above worked because the data was round blobs of similar size, which is precisely what the squared-distance objective assumes. Here is what happens when it is not, on three datasets built to violate one assumption each. In every panel k-means gets the right k handed to it and still fails, because the failure is in the geometry, not in the count.

The common thread: k-means draws straight borders equidistant between centres, so any structure that is not "round islands of similar spread" gets carved against its grain. Stretched clusters get sliced across the short axis. Rings get cut into half-moons, an ARI of -0.003 against the truth: random guessing territory. A huge diffuse cluster loses its edge to a small tight neighbour, because a mean-based centre cannot say "my cluster is wider than yours". The models that can say that, mixtures of Gaussians with their own covariances, are where this module goes next.

The Blind Test

Synthetic blobs are a rigged demo, so here is the honest one. The 178 Italian wines from the first article: thirteen chemical measurements each, standardised, and this time the cultivar column is deleted. K-means gets coordinates and the number three, and nothing else.


Then the reveal. Its three clusters are matched against the three cultivars it never saw, and they agree on 172 of the 178 bottles, an adjusted Rand index of 0.897. Six bottles sit on the wrong side of a boundary. The grouping that took an agronomist a label and a season to certify is, to a first approximation, already present in the chemistry, and two averages in a loop found it.


One honest caveat before the picture convinces you too much: this worked because the cultivars really are three roughly-round clouds in the standardised 13-dimensional space, which the previous eleven articles spent a lot of ink establishing. K-means did not discover that the data was clusterable. It confirmed it, cheaply. When it disagrees with a labelling, that is where the interesting questions start, not where they end.

The Verdict

One algorithm this time, so the table is about decisions rather than rivals.

Decision Default Why Watch out for
Initialisation k-means++, several restarts 300 measured restarts here: random hit a bad basin 8% of the time, k-means++ never did. One restart of anything is a lottery ticket, however good the opening move.
Choosing k Silhouette peak, elbow as a sanity check Inertia always falls with k; the silhouette actually peaks, here at 3 with 0.683. Both assume clusters k-means can represent. On rings every k is wrong.
Preprocessing Standardise every feature Distance adds coordinates; an unscaled feature in thousands owns the objective. The wine result needs this: raw proline alone would have decided everything.
When not to use it Non-round structure, wild size ratios, outliers Measured above: ARI 0.53 sheared, 0.00 on rings, 0.31 with unequal spreads. It will still return k tidy clusters. Nothing in the output warns you.

The deepest thing about this module is what disappeared with the answer key: the word "wrong". A supervised model can be graded; a clustering can only be judged useful, stable and consistent with what you already know. That is why every claim on this page leaned on measurements, restarts and a blind test rather than on a single score. It is also why the next article matters: k-means gives every point to exactly one cluster with total confidence, and confidence was exactly the thing this atlas has spent three articles learning to distrust. A model that can say "this point is probably-A-but-maybe-B" is called a mixture of Gaussians, and it is where the boxes of this article grow soft edges.
