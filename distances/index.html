aleetreny

Module 2.2 · Dimensionality Reduction · Scaling and Latent Variables

The previous article spent itself on one equation, \(X \approx WH\), and on what changes when you constrain \(W\) and \(H\) in three different ways. Every version of it needed the data matrix: the actual numbers, in actual columns, with an actual origin. This article asks what is left when you do not have that.


There are two honest reasons not to have it. The first is that sometimes the matrix genuinely does not exist. Nobody has ever measured the coordinates of a smell, or of a chess position, or of a written digit in any space where the axes mean something; what you can measure is how often two of them get mistaken for each other, which is a dissimilarity and nothing more. Multidimensional scaling is the machinery for turning a table of dissimilarities back into a picture, and the surprise is how little you give up: for the ordinary case it gives back exactly what PCA would have.


The second reason is that the matrix exists but lies. PCA hands its first direction to whichever column happens to vary most, and a column can vary because something interesting is happening in it or because the instrument that recorded it is noisy. PCA cannot tell those apart, because it has no model of noise and, strictly, no model of anything: it is a rotation. Factor analysis is the same picture with one extra assumption bolted on, that each variable has a private variance belonging to nobody else, and that single assumption turns a rotation into a generative model that can be fitted, tested and rejected.

Put the Origin Back

A table of distances has no origin: shifting every bottle by the same amount leaves it untouched, so the table cannot tell you where the middle is. Declaring the middle to be the average of the bottles fixes that, and doing it costs one line of arithmetic on the squared distances: subtract each row's mean, subtract each column's mean, add the grand mean back, halve and flip the sign. What comes out is a matrix of inner products rather than distances, which is a different animal entirely.

Which Is Already the Answer

Two Columns of It Is a Map

And It Is PCA's Map

Written down, the whole of classical scaling is one line, and the line is worth staring at because everything else in this section is a consequence of it. For a distance matrix \(D\) and the centring matrix \(J = I - \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\):

$$ B \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\,J\,D^{(2)}\,J \;=\; X_c X_c^{\top}, \qquad B = U\Lambda U^{\top}, \qquad Z = U_k \Lambda_k^{1/2} $$

Not Every Dissimilarity Is a Distance

That derivation assumed something it never announced: that the numbers in the table came from points in some Euclidean space in the first place. Ask people how similar two things are, or count how often a classifier mixes them up, and there is no reason on earth the answers should be consistent with any arrangement of points anywhere. The remarkable part is that the algebra tells you when they are not, for free, and to a decimal.


The eigenvalues of \(B\) are the variances of the recovered coordinates, and a variance cannot be negative. If one comes back negative, there is no configuration of points, in any number of dimensions, whose distances are the ones you handed in. So the share of the eigenvalue mass that arrives negative is a direct measurement of how far the table is from being a geometry. When Only the Order Is Real


So here is the test. Take a configuration whose answer is known, distort every distance by raising it to a power, which bends the numbers a lot and leaves their ordering untouched, and ask both methods to recover the original. Metric scaling minimises the gap between the dissimilarities and the map's distances directly. Non-metric scaling inserts one step: at every iteration it fits the best increasing staircase through the current distances, using the same pool adjacent violators algorithm the calibration article used on probabilities, and then chases the staircase instead of the data.

A Variance Is Two Things


Written as a model rather than as a rotation, the claim is that every row is a handful of hidden factors plus noise that belongs to one variable alone, so the covariance splits into a low rank part and a diagonal:

$$ x \;=\; \Lambda z + \varepsilon, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_k), \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Psi),\; \Psi \text{ diagonal} $$ $$ \operatorname{Cov}(x) \;=\; \underbrace{\Lambda \Lambda^{\top}}_{\text{shared}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Psi}_{\text{private}} $$

PCA has no \(\Psi\). It cannot have one: it has no likelihood to maximise, so it has nowhere to put the idea that some of what it sees is not worth explaining. The Verdict

Two ways of not trusting the data matrix, and they fail in opposite directions.

Decision Default Why Watch out for
You have coordinates PCA, and skip the distance matrix
You only have dissimilarities Classical scaling, then check the spectrum
The numbers are ranks, ratings or counts Non-metric MDS
Some columns are noisier than others Factor analysis, not PCA
Naming the factors Rotate, and stop pretending the axes were given
