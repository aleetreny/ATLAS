Trees and Ensembles

One question at a time: why a single tree is a bad model, and the one equation that says how many you need

Module 1.2 · Classification

Every model in this atlas so far has swallowed all of its features in one gulp and produced an answer in a single shot. A weighted sum, a distance, a dot product with a normal vector. None of them resembles how anyone actually decides anything.


A doctor does not evaluate a thirty-dimensional hyperplane. They ask a question, look at the answer, and let it decide the next question. That is a decision tree, and it is the only model in this module you could execute with a pencil.


It is also, on its own, one of the worst models here. A single tree grown to completion memorises its training set, and refitting it on a slightly different sample of the same data produces a visibly different model. The cure is to grow hundreds and average them, which sounds like the kind of thing that works because you did more of it.


It is not. There is an exact equation for how much averaging buys, it has two terms, and only one of them goes away when you add trees. That equation is why a random forest samples features and not just rows, and this article measures it on real data rather than quoting it. Along the way, two things that will lie to you: a feature importance ranking that rewards a pure-noise column 5.7 times over another pure-noise column for no reason but arithmetic, and a model that answers questions outside its training range with a flat line.


Same 569 biopsies as the rest of this branch, from logistic regression onward, so every number here is directly comparable to the ones there.

Twenty Questions

Two features, so the model can be drawn as what it literally is: a set of boxes. Every split is a horizontal or vertical cut, every leaf is a rectangle, and the prediction inside a rectangle is whatever the majority of the training points in it were.

398 Biopsies

Perimeter across, concavity up. The malignant ones gather at the top right and the benign ones at the bottom left, with a contested band in between. No model yet, just the room the model has to carve up.

One Cut

The tree considers every threshold on every feature and keeps the one that separates best. Here it is worst concave points at 0.142, which takes a room that was 45% malignant and produces two rooms that are 6% and 89%. That single question does most of the work.

Then Recurse

Now the same procedure runs inside each box, independently, and knows nothing about the other side. That is what greedy means here: the first cut was chosen without any idea what would be possible afterwards, and it is never revisited.

Depth Three

Eight boxes. The boundary is starting to trace the shape of the contested band, and held-out accuracy is at its peak for this pair of features. This is roughly as good as one tree gets here.

Depth Five

Eighteen boxes, several of them slivers holding two or three points. Training accuracy has climbed to 0.985 and held-out accuracy has fallen to 0.901. Every one of those slivers is a rule about one patient.

Unpruned

Twenty-six boxes and a perfect score on the training set. There is no error left to reduce, so the tree stops, having produced a map of exactly the 398 people it was shown. Held-out accuracy: 0.889, the worst of any depth.

How a Question Gets Chosen

Everything above rests on one word: "separates best". Making it precise needs a number for how mixed a group is. The usual one is Gini impurity, which is the probability that two points drawn at random from the group have different labels:

$$ G \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k} p_k^2 $$

A pure group scores 0. A fifty-fifty mix scores 0.5, its worst. A candidate split is then graded by how much impurity it removes, weighting each side by how many points landed in it:

$$ \Delta G \;=\; G_{\text{parent}} - \frac{n_L}{n}G_L - \frac{n_R}{n}G_R $$

The tree tries every threshold on every feature and keeps the largest \(\Delta G\). That is the entire learning algorithm, applied recursively. There is no gradient, no objective being globally minimised, and no guarantee that a locally best first question leads anywhere good.

The Same Data, A Different Model

Here is the property that makes a single tree unusable and every ensemble in this article necessary. Take the same 398 patients, resample them with replacement, and refit. Nothing about the problem changed. The model did.


Press through eight bootstrap samples. The boxes rearrange, thresholds jump by whole units, and held-out accuracy swings between 0.901 and 0.936. A model this sensitive to which rows it happened to see is a model whose output carries a large random component, and that component has a name: variance.


It is worth being clear about why trees in particular have so much of it. The split at the root is chosen by a comparison between candidates that are often nearly tied. Change a handful of rows, a different candidate wins by a hair, and every subsequent question in the entire tree is now being asked inside a different partition. The greediness that makes a tree fast is exactly what makes it unstable.

What Averaging Actually Buys

The fix is obvious enough that Breiman's 1996 paper is mostly about how well it works rather than why anyone would try it: grow many trees on many bootstrap samples and average their votes. Bagging, for bootstrap aggregating.


What is not obvious is how much it buys, and that has an exact answer. Take \(B\) predictors, each with variance \(\sigma^2\), with correlation \(\rho\) between any two of them. The variance of their average is:

$$ \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} T_b\right) \;=\; \rho\,\sigma^2 \;+\; \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 $$

Read the two terms. The second is divided by \(B\), so it vanishes: that is the free lunch, and it is why more trees never hurt. The first has no \(B\) in it at all. No number of trees removes it. Averaging a thousand copies of nearly the same opinion gives you nearly the same opinion.


Which turns the whole design problem into one question: how do you make the trees disagree? Bootstrapping the rows is one answer, and Breiman's second idea was better. At every split, hide most of the columns and force the tree to choose from a random handful. A tree that is not allowed to see the strongest feature has to find another way, and the forest stops being a thousand near-copies of the same greedy first question. That is a random forest, and extra trees pushes further still by not even optimising the threshold, just picking one at random.


Everything below is measured rather than asserted. Twenty independent training sets, one forest grown on each, and then the spread of their predictions compared against what the equation says it should be. Neither \(\rho\) nor \(\sigma^2\) is fitted to the curve: both are read off the trees, and the curve is the prediction.

Three things worth pulling out of that chart.


The equation works. With no free parameters it tracks the measured variance to within 9% for bagging and 8% for the random forest across three orders of magnitude of \(B\). At 200 trees the forest's predicted variance is 0.001218 and the measured one is 0.001215.


Sampling columns does what it was designed to do. Bagging's trees correlate at \(\rho = 0.053\). Hide most of the columns at every split and that falls to 0.018, which drops the floor from 0.00263 to 0.00096. Nothing else about the algorithm changed. That single intervention is the difference between bagging and a random forest, and the equation says exactly what it is worth.


And you pay for it. Look at \(\sigma^2\) as you move down the three: 0.0498, 0.0527, 0.0555. Each individual tree gets worse as you handicap it, because a tree forced to split on a random subset of columns is not choosing the best question available. The trade is a slightly worse committee member in exchange for a less redundant committee, and on this data it is worth taking: one tree averages 0.912 and the forest of 200 reaches 0.949.


One honest caveat. On 569 rows with a strong signal the floor is small in absolute terms, so most of the improvement here comes from the second term, the one that was always going to vanish. The floor matters much more when the trees are deep, the signal is weak, or one feature dominates so hard that every tree opens with the same question. That case is exactly what feature sampling was invented for.

More Is Never Worse

One consequence of that equation deserves its own heading, because it is the sharpest contrast in this whole atlas.


In gradient boosting, every tree is fitted to the previous ensemble's mistakes, so adding trees keeps changing the model and eventually overfits: the number of trees is a regularisation parameter you must tune, with a held-out curve that turns back upward.


A forest has no such curve. Its trees are independent draws from the same distribution, so adding one more only ever moves the average closer to its own limit. B is not a hyperparameter, it is a compute budget. On this data the log loss falls from 1.818 at one tree to 0.227 at fifty and then simply stops moving: 0.2263 at two hundred, 0.2263 at four hundred. Nothing turns around, because there is nothing left to turn.


Press the accuracy button and you will catch the one honest wrinkle in that sentence: 0.9532 at a hundred trees, 0.9474 at two hundred. That is a single patient of the 171 crossing the 0.5 threshold as the averaged probabilities settle, and it is a fact about thresholds, not about forests. The probabilities themselves, which the log loss watches, never look back. "Never worse" is a statement in expectation, and on any one split a coin can land oddly once.

The Importance Ranking Is Not What You Think

A forest hands you a feature importance ranking for free, and it is one of the most widely quoted numbers in applied machine learning. The default one, mean decrease in impurity, adds up the \(\Delta G\) of every split made on a feature, weighted by how many samples passed through. It is fast, it comes with the model, and it has a bias that is easy to state and easy to demonstrate.


A feature with more distinct values offers more candidate thresholds. More candidates means more chances that one of them, by luck alone, splits the training data slightly better than nothing. That lucky split earns a \(\Delta G\) which goes into the total. The more granular a column is, the more importance it can accumulate from pure chance.


So here is the experiment. Five extra columns were added to the 30 real ones, and every single one is random noise, independent of the diagnosis. They differ only in how many distinct values they take: 2, 4, 10, 50, and one per patient. Any importance any of them receives is undeserved by construction. Watch what happens.

The five noise columns score 0.00053, 0.00056, 0.00115, 0.00159 and 0.00304. They contain exactly the same amount of information about the diagnosis, which is none, and the finest-grained one is rated 5.7 times more important than the coarsest. The ranking is measuring granularity.


Two things stop this from being a scandal, and both are worth saying so the warning stays proportionate. First, on this data no noise column beats even the weakest real feature, which scores 0.00332: the bias is real and it is not large enough here to overturn anything. Second, there is a fix. Permutation importance asks a different question, and a much better one: shuffle a column on the held-out set and measure how much accuracy actually falls. A column that contributes nothing loses nothing when scrambled, and all five planted columns duly score essentially zero. It costs a refit-free pass per feature and it answers the question people think they are asking.


The failure mode this predicts is concrete and common: a user ID, a timestamp, a postcode, a free-text hash. High cardinality, no signal, and a confident position near the top of a chart somebody is about to put in a slide.

The Wall at the Edge of the Data

One last property, and it follows from the very first picture in this article. A tree's prediction is constant inside each box. The boxes at the edge of the training data are unbounded, so the prediction out there is constant too, forever.


A tree cannot extrapolate. Not badly: at all. Below is a clean upward trend, with the model trained only on the left portion and then asked about the right. The straight line, which assumed a shape and can therefore be wrong in an interesting way, tracks the truth out to the edge. The tree and the forest return the last value they ever learned and hold it flat to the horizon.

This is not a bug and it is not fixable by adding trees, because averaging a thousand flat lines gives a flat line. It is the direct consequence of a model made of boxes, and it is the single most important thing to know before putting a forest anywhere near a trend, a time series, or a price that has never been this high before.

The Verdict

Five models, one dataset, five-fold cross validation over all 569 biopsies.

Model 5-fold accuracy Reach for it when… Watch out for
One tree, unpruned 0.9244 ± 0.0145 You need a model a human can read out loud and act on without a computer. It memorises. Refit on a resample and you get a visibly different model.
One tree, depth 4 0.9209 ± 0.0200 Same, plus you want the rules to fit on one page. Depth is a real hyperparameter here, unlike the number of trees below.
Bagging 0.9525 ± 0.0207 Rarely, now. It is the idea random forests improved on. Every tree opens with the same question, so ρ stays high.
Random forest 0.9596 ± 0.0120 Almost always, as the baseline before anything clever. Two knobs, both forgiving. Cannot extrapolate. Its default importances reward granularity.
Extra trees 0.9666 ± 0.0151 Same as above, faster to fit, and worth trying whenever a forest is. Random thresholds mean each tree is weaker; it needs the ensemble more.

The through-line of this module so far: one dataset, and three completely different ways to draw a line through it. A probability, a wall, and a stack of boxes. All three land between 0.93 and 0.97, which is itself the lesson: on clean tabular data the choice of model family matters far less than most tutorials imply, and what separates them is everything else. Whether the probability means anything. Whether the boundary can curve. Whether the answer survives a resample.
