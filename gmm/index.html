Gaussian Mixtures

Clusters with soft edges: the model that says "probably this one, but maybe that one", and learns every group's shape

aleetreny

Module 2.1 · Clustering · Mixtures

The previous article ended with two confessions. K-means hands every point to exactly one cluster with total confidence, and it draws every territory as a round island. Both defects have the same cure, and it is one idea, not two: stop assigning points to clusters, and start asking which mixture of overlapping populations most plausibly generated this data.


A Gaussian mixture model says the data came from \(k\) Gaussian bells, each with its own centre, its own shape (a full covariance matrix, so any tilt and stretch), and its own share of the data. Under that story, a point near the boundary of two bells is not a hard call to make. It is a probability to report: 60% this cultivar, 40% that one. The soft answer is not a hedge, it is extra information, and this article measures exactly what it is worth.


Everything runs on data the previous article already measured: the same 300 blobs, the same failure gallery, the same 178 wines. The algorithm, expectation-maximisation, runs live in this page's JavaScript, checked at load time against scikit-learn. You will drag its starting guesses around with your own hands, freeze its soft answers into k-means with a slider, and watch the failure gallery get two redemptions and one honest refusal.

Two Steps, Again, But Softer

The same 300 points. This time the three guesses are not just centres: each is a full Gaussian, drawn below as its 1σ and 2σ ellipses, starting as three overlapping shrugs in the middle of the data.

Guess Three Bells

A mixture is a recipe: three Gaussians, each with a centre, a covariance (the ellipse), and a weight (its share of the data). These three start deliberately crowded in the middle, all shape and no opinion, so you can watch opinion form.

E-step: Share, Don't Assign

For every point, Bayes' rule asks: given where the bells are, what share of this point belongs to each? No point is handed over whole. The colour of every dot is the literal blend of its shares, and in this crowded start almost everything is a muddy mixture, which is the honest description of a bad model.

M-step: Refit to the Shares

Each Gaussian refits itself to all the points, weighted by its shares: weighted mean, weighted covariance, weighted share of the data. Where k-means moved a centre, this moves a centre, reshapes an ellipse and renegotiates a weight, all in one step of arithmetic.

Climb

Share, refit, share, refit. Each pass can only ever raise the data's log-likelihood under the model, the mirror image of inertia only ever falling, and for the same reason: both steps are exact best-responses to the other's output. Watch the tints separate as the bells pull apart.

Converged, and Checked

The mixture lands within a whisker of the truth (ARI 0.980 against the generating labels) and this browser's final log-likelihood matches scikit-learn's answer to the fourth decimal. Note what the tints keep that k-means threw away: the points between two bells still wear their doubt.

What Is Being Climbed

The quantity is the log-likelihood of the data under the mixture:

$$ \log L \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{c=1}^{k} \pi_c \, \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_i \,\big|\, \mu_c, \Sigma_c\big) $$

The sum inside the log is what makes this hard: nobody knows which bell produced which point. EM's move is to treat those memberships as the missing information, estimate them from the current model (E-step), then refit the model as if they were known (M-step). Each step is exact, each pass climbs, and the loop stops at a local maximum, with the same fine print as Lloyd's loop: a local maximum, not the one. The lottery of the first guess did not go away when the assignments went soft. So run it yourself.

Those diamonds are yours: drag them anywhere, then run. Every run animates this page's own EM, iteration by iteration, and the readout compares your landing against scikit-learn's optimum. Most starts make it. Some do not, and when they do not, the failure is silent: the likelihood still climbed, the ellipses still settled, and only the comparison against a better run gives it away. Which is why real fits restart several times and keep the best, exactly as k-means did.

K-Means Is a Frozen Mixture

The relationship between the two articles is not an analogy. Take the converged mixture's responsibilities, raise every one to the power \(1/T\), renormalise, and lower the temperature \(T\) toward zero: every share snaps to 0 or 1, every point goes wholly to its likeliest bell, and the picture becomes the previous article. K-means is this model with the thermostat at zero (and the ellipses forced round: the next section prices that part). Slide it yourself and watch the doubt freeze out.

The Covariance Wardrobe

The other half of the promise: shape. Scikit-learn offers four constraints on \(\Sigma_c\), from "circles only" to "anything goes", and the sheared blobs that humiliated k-means (ARI 0.529) are the perfect judge of what each is worth. Two results below deserve staring at. Spherical scores 0.509: a mixture with round covariances is k-means with paperwork. And the winner by BIC is not the freest model but tied: all three clusters sharing one learned shape. These blobs really were sheared by a single matrix, tied says exactly that with six fewer parameters, and the criterion rewards the model that matches how the data was actually made.

How Many Bells

Choosing \(k\) tortured the previous article: inertia falls forever, so the elbow is a squint, and the silhouette had to be imported as a second opinion. A likelihood model carries its own answer. The Bayesian information criterion is the fit's log-likelihood charged for every parameter it spends (\(\mathrm{BIC} = p\ln n - 2\log L\), lower is better), and unlike inertia it genuinely bottoms out: on these blobs at k = 3 with 2,339, rising again to 2,368 at k = 4. Slide k and watch the criterion refuse ellipses the eye might have accepted.

k = 3

The Failure Gallery, Revisited

The three datasets built to break k-means, refit with full-covariance mixtures and the same k as before. Two of the three failures were never about the data at all: give the model a vocabulary for shape and for unequal spread, and the stretched clusters go from ARI 0.529 to 1.000, the mismatched pair from 0.306 to 0.925. The rings stay broken, 0.002, and they should: a weighted sum of bells is still bells. That refusal is the honest edge of this whole family of models, and the next article walks over it.

The Blind Test, With Doubt Allowed

The 178 wines again, all 13 standardised dimensions, labels deleted, three components. One modelling decision first: in 13 dimensions a full covariance per cluster costs 314 parameters against 178 bottles, and it overfits (ARI 0.880). BIC picks the diagonal family at 80 parameters, and that model scores ARI 0.915, 173 of 178 bottles: one better than k-means on every count, from the same information.


The bottle count is not the point, though. The point is the five bottles the mixture is unsure about, because now unsureness is an output. Among the 173 bottles holding at least 90% membership in one cluster, the model agrees with the cultivar label 98.3% of the time. Among the five below that bar: 60%. The doubt lands exactly where the errors live, which is what it is for.

The Verdict

One model family, so the table is about the decisions it demands.

Decision Default Why Watch out for
Covariance family Let BIC choose among all four Measured twice here: tied beat full on the sheared blobs, diagonal beat full on the wine. Freedom is not free. Full covariance in d dimensions costs k·d(d+1)/2 parameters; on small data it memorises.
Choosing k BIC minimum, several restarts per k An actual minimum (2,339 at k = 3 here), against inertia's endless slide. BIC assumes the data is roughly a mixture of Gaussians. On rings, every k is wrong.
Initialisation Multiple restarts, keep the best likelihood The drag widget is the proof: some starts land in worse optima, silently. A component can also collapse onto a few points; the ridge on the covariance is not optional.
Using the output Keep the probabilities, not just the argmax On the wine: 98.3% right when sure, 60% when hesitant. The hesitations are a free triage list. They are model beliefs, not calibrated frequencies; the calibration article's warnings apply.
When not to use it Non-blob structure Rings: 0.002. A sum of bells cannot draw a hole. It will still converge, report tidy ellipses and raise no alarm. Nothing in the output warns you.

Every model so far in this module has answered "which cluster?" with geometry: distances to centres, bells around means. The rings are the standing rebuke, twice unfixed, and they point at the missing idea. What makes both rings feel like two groups is not their shape and not any centre. It is that each ring is dense inside and empty around. The next article starts from that observation alone, defines a cluster as a connected region of high density, and the rings fall in one move, along with two questions this article quietly dodged: what to do with points that belong to no cluster, and how to find clusters when nobody, not even BIC, will tell you k.
