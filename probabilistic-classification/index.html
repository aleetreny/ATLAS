Probabilistic Classification

Logistic regression, naive Bayes, and the difference between being right and being believable

aleetreny

Module 1.2 · Classification

Six articles of this atlas have asked how much. How many miles per gallon, how many dollars, how many diners. The answer was a number, and the score was how far off it was. This module asks a different question: which one. Benign or malignant. Fraud or not. Churn or stay. The answer is a category, and the moment that changes, so does everything about what counts as a good model.


Here is the part that gets skipped. A classifier could just hand you the label and stop. Most of the interesting ones hand you a probability instead, and a probability is a promise: of all the cases where I said seventy percent, seventy percent of them will turn out positive. That promise is checkable. You can hold a model to it after the fact and find out whether it was lying.


Almost nobody checks. Accuracy gets reported, the model ships, and its probabilities go straight into a decision, a triage queue, a price. This article builds the two foundational probabilistic classifiers, and then does the checking. The two of them will land within 4.7 points of each other on accuracy and a factor of ten apart on the probabilities, and the second number is the one that would hurt you.


The data is 569 breast biopsies from Wisconsin [1], digitised into 30 measurements of cell nuclei: radius, texture, concavity, and so on, each summarised three ways. 212 of the samples are malignant. It is a small, clean, famous dataset, and it has a property we will lean on hard later: several of its features are the same quantity measured under different names.

A Line Through Zeros and Ones

Start with the single most informative measurement of the thirty. Every dot is a biopsy: horizontal position is the measurement, vertical position is the answer, which can only be 0 or 1. There is no cloud here, just two rails, and the whole problem is what to draw between them.

Two Rails

398 training biopsies. The benign ones sit on the floor at y = 0, the malignant ones on the ceiling at y = 1. Even by eye the measurement separates them: small on the left, large on the right, with an argument in the middle.

Do the Obvious Thing

We have a tool for drawing a line through points. Least squares does not know or care that the target only takes two values, so hand it the zeros and ones and see what comes back. It slopes upward, it passes between the rails, and at a glance it looks like a reasonable answer.

Read It Out Loud

Now treat the height of that line as a probability, which is what you would have to do to use it. At the far left it predicts −0.22, and at the far right 1.99. Seventy-four of the 398 training biopsies get a probability that does not exist. A straight line has no ceiling and no floor, and probabilities have both.

Bend the Ends

So we need a function that rises with the measurement, is free in the middle, and cannot leave the interval no matter how far you push it. There is one obvious candidate shape: an S. Flat, then steep, then flat again. The sigmoid is that shape, and the next section shows that it is not a shape somebody liked, it is the only one consistent with a linear model of the odds.

The Same Ranking

Here they are together. Notice what did not change: both curves order these 398 patients identically, and the area under the ROC curve is 0.981 for each of them, to four decimals. On a single feature the straight line and the S are the same classifier. They differ only in the numbers they report, which is exactly the distinction this article is about.

Where the Decision Lives

The curve crosses 0.5 at one place, and that single point is the entire decision rule: to the right, malignant. Everything the model knows about the boundary is one number. Everything it knows about doubt is the steepness of the curve at that point, and that is the part a hard label throws away.

The Only Function That Fits

The sigmoid is usually introduced as a convenient squashing function, which makes it look arbitrary. It is not. It falls out of one modelling decision, and the decision is easier to defend than the curve.


Probabilities are a bad thing to be linear in, because they are trapped between 0 and 1. Odds are not. The odds of an event are \(p / (1-p)\): a probability of 0.5 is odds of 1, a probability of 0.9 is odds of 9, and as \(p\) approaches 1 the odds run off to infinity. That fixes the ceiling but not the floor, since odds cannot go below zero. Take the logarithm and the floor goes too:

$$ \operatorname{logit}(p) \;=\; \log\frac{p}{1-p} \;\in\; (-\infty, +\infty) $$

Now there is something a linear model can safely predict. So state the model in the only place where a straight line is not an embarrassment:

$$ \log\frac{p}{1-p} \;=\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_d x_d $$

Everything else is algebra. Solve that for \(p\) and the sigmoid is what comes out, not because anyone chose it but because there is nothing else it could be:

$$ p \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta^{\top}x)}} \;=\; \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta^{\top}x) $$


This also hands you the interpretation for free, and it is a better one than linear regression gets. A coefficient is not "how much the probability moves", because that depends on where you start. It is how much the odds multiply, and that is the same everywhere. A coefficient of \(\beta_j\) means one standard deviation more of feature \(j\) multiplies the odds of malignancy by \(e^{\beta_j}\), whether you were at ten percent or at ninety.

Why Not Squared Error

We have a shape. We still need a way to score it, and the obvious move is to reuse the one from the last six articles: take the squared distance between the predicted probability and the true 0 or 1, and minimise it. It is a perfectly sensible-looking objective. It is also the wrong one, and the reason is worth seeing rather than being told.


The right objective comes from asking the model to explain the data it actually saw. If the model says \(p\) and the truth was 1, the probability it assigned to what happened is \(p\); if the truth was 0, it is \(1-p\). Multiply that over every patient, take the log to turn the product into a sum, and negate it so we can minimise:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[\, y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \,\Big] $$

That is cross-entropy, and it is the same maximum-likelihood machinery the Poisson GLM used in the first article: a different distribution for the target, the same principle for fitting it. Its gradient is the same old friend again, prediction minus truth.


Below, both objectives over the same two coefficients, drawn as contour maps. Press the toggle and watch what happens to the shape of the ground. Cross-entropy is convex: one basin, every path downhill reaches the same bottom. Squared error through a sigmoid is not, and the plateau it grows across half the map is where a gradient descent goes to die. Drop the ball in the same place on both and compare where it ends up: from the bottom-left corner, cross-entropy travels 13.8 units and lands exactly on the answer, while squared error moves 3.1 units, stops, and sits at 0.37 on a surface whose floor is 0.05.

Click anywhere on the map to start a descent from there. Both runs use the same learning rate and the same number of steps.

Two Ways to Answer the Same Question

Logistic regression never asks what a malignant tumour looks like. It only asks where the boundary between the two groups goes, and it spends all of its capacity on that boundary. Models built this way are called discriminative.


There is an entirely different route to the same answer. Instead of modelling the boundary, model each class: what does the distribution of radii look like among malignant tumours, and separately among benign ones? Once you have those, Bayes' theorem turns them around for you:

$$ \underbrace{P(y \mid x)}_{\text{what you want}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{P(x \mid y)}^{\text{what each class looks like}} \; \overbrace{P(y)}^{\text{how common it is}}}{P(x)} $$

Models built this way are generative: they carry a description of the data complete enough that you could draw new samples from it. They know more, in principle. The catch is what it takes to know it. A full joint distribution over 30 features is a 30-dimensional object, and 398 training patients is nowhere near enough to pin one down.


Naive Bayes solves that with one brutal assumption: given the class, every feature is independent of every other. The 30-dimensional distribution collapses into thirty one-dimensional ones, and thirty one-dimensional Gaussians are trivially easy to estimate.

$$ P(x \mid y) \;\approx\; \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(x_j \mid y) $$


That is the whole model. Two numbers per feature per class, a mean and a standard deviation, and the training is done in one pass with no optimisation at all. Below are the bells it fits: pick a feature and see what naive Bayes believes about it.

One Number Wearing Three Hats

"Given the class, every feature is independent of every other" is famously false, and everybody says so before moving on. It is worth not moving on, because on this dataset the assumption is not approximately violated. It is violated in the most complete way available.


Three of the thirty features are mean radius, mean perimeter and mean area. For anything roughly circular those are one measurement written three ways, and the training set agrees: radius and perimeter correlate at 0.998, and each of them with area at 0.986. Knowing one tells you the others to within rounding.


Naive Bayes does not know that, and its arithmetic has no way to find out. It multiplies the three likelihoods as though they were three separate witnesses, so a large nucleus gets to testify three times. Do that across thirty features with several such families and the log-posterior is not slightly too confident. It is compounded too confident, and the compounding is exponential in the number of duplicated columns.


One important thing this does not wreck is the ranking. Multiplying the evidence by a roughly constant factor stretches the scores without reordering them, which is why naive Bayes keeps winning benchmarks that only measure ordering. Watch it happen on two features that are nearly independent, where the assumption is close to true and the two models produce almost the same picture.

Where the Assumption Sends the Bill

Two features, barely correlated, and the two models are indistinguishable. Now give them all thirty, correlated families and all, and score four different things: whether the label is right, whether the ordering is right, and two measures of whether the number is right.


The two probability scores are worth a sentence each. Brier is just the mean squared error of the probability against the 0 or 1 that happened, so it charges you gently and symmetrically for being off. Log loss is the same cross-entropy the model was trained on, and it charges you unboundedly for being confident and wrong: predict 0.999 for something that does not happen and it costs about seven, where a shrugging 0.5 would have cost 0.7.


The third row is the control. QDA, which the next article builds properly, is Gaussian naive Bayes with the covariances put back in: same Gaussians, same Bayes' theorem, the only difference being that it is allowed to know the features move together. Naive Bayes is literally QDA with the off-diagonal terms of every covariance set to zero.


One caveat, because it is itself part of the argument. On thirty features and 398 rows, un-shrunk QDA cannot be fitted at all: each class has fewer rows than the 465 numbers its covariance matrix contains, the matrix is singular, and the fit throws. The row below uses a 5% shrink toward a scaled identity to make it exist. So the control is not perfectly clean, and the reason it is not clean is exactly why the naive assumption was invented. The conclusion does not depend on the amount: at 1%, 5% and 10% shrinkage the Brier score comes out 0.043, 0.034 and 0.033, against naive Bayes at 0.062 for all of them.

Accuracy differs by 4.7 points. Ranking differs by fourteen thousandths of an AUC, which on a five-fold check is inside the noise. Log loss differs by a factor of ten. Putting the covariances back recovers most of that gap, which is about as close to a controlled experiment as this kind of thing gets: the naive assumption costs almost nothing in who and almost everything in how sure.


The obvious repair is to delete the duplicated columns, and it is worth reporting that it does not work. Dropping every feature correlated above 0.9 with one already kept takes the model from 30 columns to 21, and its Brier score gets worse, from 0.062 to 0.086, because the columns being deleted carried real signal alongside the duplication. The problem is not the columns. It is the assumption, and you cannot fix an assumption by throwing away data.

Checking the Promise

Log loss says something is wrong. It does not say what. For that there is one picture, and it is the most underused diagnostic in applied machine learning.


Take the held-out patients, sort them by the probability the model gave them, and put them into buckets: everyone told "under ten percent", everyone told "between ten and twenty", and so on. Within each bucket, compare what the model promised against what actually happened. A model whose probabilities mean what they say lands on the diagonal in every bucket. That property has a name, calibration, and it is logically independent of accuracy: you can be perfectly calibrated and useless, or extremely accurate and a liar.


Both models here are accurate. Only one of them is telling the truth.

Read the bottom-left bucket, because that is the one that matters in a hospital. Naive Bayes put 108 of the 171 held-out patients in it, with an average predicted probability of malignancy of 0.00024. Seven of those 108 had cancer. The model did not say "unlikely". It said, to three decimal places, no, and it said it to seven people who had a malignant tumour.


Logistic regression put 98 patients in the same bucket at an average of 0.010, and one of them was malignant. That is what a probability looks like when it is doing its job: not more accurate, just honest about the size of the doubt. Across the whole test set, naive Bayes is past 99% certainty on 97% of patients. It has essentially two outputs, yes and no, wearing the costume of a probability.


None of this makes naive Bayes a bad algorithm. It is fast, it needs almost no data, it handles hundreds of thousands of features without complaint, and on text, where the features really are closer to independent, it is a respectable baseline to this day. The failure is specific and predictable: use its ranking, do not quote its probability.

Nobody Voted for 0.5

One last thing a calibrated probability buys you, and it is the reason to care about all of the above.


Turning a probability into a decision needs a threshold, and 0.5 arrives by default with no argument behind it. It is only correct if the two mistakes cost the same. Here, one mistake is a healthy person sent for a second test, and the other is a malignant tumour told to go home. Those are not the same mistake, and no amount of model quality will notice that on its own, because the cost lives outside the data.


Below is the logistic model's held-out performance at every threshold. Slide it and watch the two kinds of error trade against each other. At 0.40 the model misses two cancers and raises one false alarm; push it down to 0.06 and it misses none, at a cost of twelve. Whether that is a good trade is not a question about the model. It is a question about what a missed cancer is worth, and the second slider makes you answer it.


The point is not to find the best threshold. The point is that the choice is yours, it lives outside the data where no amount of accuracy can reach it, and the only reason you can make it at all is that the number the model produced meant something.

The Verdict

Two models, one question, and a split that is sharper than the usual accuracy table suggests.

Model Reach for it when… It assumes… Trained by Watch out for
Logistic regression You need a probability anyone is going to act on, and you want to read the coefficients out loud. The log-odds are linear in the features. Maximising likelihood, on a convex surface with no local minima. Separable data sends the coefficients to infinity. Regularise, always.
Gaussian naive Bayes Features vastly outnumber rows, training has to be instant, or you only need a ranking. Every feature is independent of every other, given the class. Counting. One pass, two moments per feature per class, no optimiser. Correlated features. It will still classify well and its probabilities will be worthless.
QDA The classes really are roughly Gaussian and you have enough rows per class to estimate a covariance. Each class is a Gaussian, with its own covariance. Counting again, plus one covariance matrix per class. Needs samples growing with the square of the feature count. Next article.

The thread through all of it: a classifier that hands you a label has answered a smaller question than the one you asked. The probability is where the useful information lives, and it is also the part that fails quietly, because nothing in a standard evaluation will tell you it has.


Next in this module, we throw probability away entirely. If two groups of points sit in space, there are infinitely many walls you could build between them, and one of them is further from everybody than the rest. That story is called Geometric Classification.
