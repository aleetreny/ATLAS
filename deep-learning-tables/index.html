Deep Learning for Tables

The architecture that took images and language keeps losing to a pile of trees

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression

Between 2012 and 2020, deep learning took every field it touched. Image classification, speech, translation, protein structure, text generation. Each one fell to roughly the same recipe: stack differentiable layers, feed them enough data, and let gradient descent find the features that decades of hand engineering had missed.


Then there are spreadsheets. Rows and columns, a few dozen features with names like carat and table %, the most common data in the world by a wide margin. On this data the recipe stalls. Not catastrophically, and not everywhere, but reliably enough that after a decade of dedicated architectures the practical advice has not changed: start with gradient boosting.


This page is about why. It is easy to write something flattering here and easy to write something dismissive, so almost everything below is a measurement rather than a claim. The same 53,920 diamonds from the previous article, the same split, seven models, and three experiments designed to answer the question rather than decorate it.


The short version, and the rest of the page is the evidence: a plain neural network gets within 25% of boosting and takes twenty times longer to get there. Two structural reasons explain most of that gap, and both can be demonstrated in a few lines. And the genuinely valuable contribution of the deep learning era to tabular data turns out not to be accuracy at all.

A Room Seen From an Angle

Start with the sharpest of the two reasons. It comes from Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux [1], it takes about ten lines to run, and once you have seen it you cannot unsee it.


Below is a made-up problem with a real property: its structure lines up with the axes. Both models in the panel are written from scratch and fitted in your browser as you scroll, so every number in the score box was measured on your machine just now.

Nine Blocks

Two features, and a label that depends only on which band of each feature a point falls into. Nine blocks in a checkerboard. This is a caricature of tabular data, and the caricature is deliberate: real columns behave like this more often than not, because thresholds are how humans record the world. Under 30. Over 65. Grade B or better.

A Tree Eats This For Breakfast

A depth-6 tree, fitted live. It recovers the blocks almost exactly, because its whole vocabulary is is this feature above that value, and the truth is written in precisely that vocabulary. Four cuts would have been enough. Note the error in the panel on the right.

Now Turn The Room

The same points, rotated by 35 degrees. Think carefully about what just happened: nothing was added, nothing was removed, and no two points changed their distance from each other. A rotation is invertible. Any model that reasons about how far apart things are is looking at an identical problem.

The Tree Cannot Follow

Refitted on the rotated data, at the same depth. Every boundary that used to be one cut is now a staircase, and a staircase costs splits that are no longer available for anything else. The error goes up by a factor of nine. The tree did not get worse at its job. Its job stopped matching the shape of the answer.

No, It Was Not The Depth Limit

The obvious objection is that depth 6 was too mean, so here is the same fit with the limit taken off entirely. It helps, and it does not rescue anything: still six times the aligned error, from a tree that now needs more than twice as many leaves to get there. Look at the row above it. The aligned tree never wanted the extra depth. It used thirteen leaves and stopped, because thirteen was enough.

Distance Did Not Change

A 15-nearest-neighbour vote on the same rotated data, from the article on non-parametric regression. Its error before and after the rotation is identical, to the decimal, and it never had to be told about the rotation. That is what rotation invariance buys, and a neural network with a dense first layer has it for the same reason: it can absorb the rotation into its weights.

So there is the trade. Rotation invariance sounds like an unambiguous good, the way translation invariance is an unambiguous good for images. On spreadsheets it is the opposite. The columns of a table are not an arbitrary basis that happens to have been chosen: each one is a quantity somebody decided to measure, and the target usually depends on those quantities individually. A model that treats the axes as sacred is exploiting real information. A model that treats them as arbitrary is throwing that information away, politely, at every layer.

The Same Test, On Diamonds

The quilt was a caricature. Here is the identical experiment on the real data: nine standardised columns multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix, same training rows, same held-out rows, same models.

The prediction holds. Boosting is the only model that cares, and it cares a lot. But read the absolute view before drawing the obvious conclusion, because it contains the correction that most retellings of this result leave out: boosting after being handicapped is still more accurate than the network was before. Rotation invariance explains where the tree's advantage comes from. It does not hand the advantage to anyone else.

Burying The Signal

The second structural reason is less elegant and, in practice, more expensive. Real datasets are full of columns that mean nothing: an ID that leaked in, a field nobody filled, a measurement that turned out not to matter. Nobody knows in advance which ones they are. So: keep the nine real columns, add columns of pure noise, and watch.

noise columns

Sixty columns of nothing cost boosting 4%. The network is 63% worse at the worst point on the curve. The mechanism is not subtle. A split is chosen by scanning every column and keeping whichever one reduces the loss most, so a column that reduces nothing is quietly never selected, and the cost of carrying it is a little compute. A dense layer has no equivalent veto. It begins by mixing all sixty-nine columns into every hidden unit and has to learn its way back out, using the same finite gradient signal it needed for the actual problem.


One honest note about that curve: the network's error at 60 junk columns is lower than at 30. That is not a pattern, it is the variance of a single run with early stopping, and the same instability shows up as the ± 28.7 on its benchmark row below. Reading a trend into non-monotonic points from one seed is how a lot of published tabular comparisons go wrong.

Seven Models, One Picture

Now the whole field at once. Fit time runs left to right, held-out error runs bottom to top, so better is down and cheaper is left. Every point is an average over independent seeds, and the vertical bar is one standard deviation across those seeds, which on one model is large enough to be the story.

Four things in that picture are worth saying out loud.


The neural models are not embarrassed. A plain multi-layer perceptron on standardised columns reaches $660, which is far closer to boosting than to the linear baseline at $1,202. Anyone who tells you networks cannot do tabular data is overstating it. They can. They just do not win.


TabNet's spread is its headline. Its mean is $700, respectable, but the standard deviation across seeds is $100, roughly fourteen percent of its own score. Boosting's is $2. A model whose result moves by a hundred dollars depending on the random seed is a model you cannot trust a single run of, and single runs are what benchmark tables are usually made of.


Nothing neural is fast. The horizontal axis is logarithmic for a reason. Press the button to see it linear: six of the seven models collapse onto the left edge and TabNet sits alone at forty seconds, roughly fifty times the cost of the model that beats it. On this dataset that is the difference between an experiment you run and an experiment you think about running.


And the interesting result is the blue one. Explainable boosting with pairwise terms lands at $588, within twelve percent of the winner, and it is a model whose every component can be plotted and read. Which brings us to the part of this story that is genuinely a contribution.

Nine Curves That Add Up

Here is the idea worth the trouble. A neural additive model [2] gives each feature its own small network, shows that network nothing but its one feature, and adds the outputs together:

$$ F(x) = \beta + \sum_{j=1}^{p} f_j(x_j) $$

Every \(f_j\) is a genuine neural network, trained end to end by the same gradient descent as any other, and free to learn any shape at all: a bend, a plateau, a cliff. What it is not free to do is look at another feature. That single restriction is what turns a black box into a picture, because a function of one variable can simply be drawn.

Pick a real stone from the held-out set and watch the nine dots move:

The bar chart is not an explanation of the model. It is the model. Because the network is additive by construction, the prediction genuinely is the average stone plus nine numbers, and the generator that produced this page checks that identity to the cent before exporting. Compare that to what you normally get from a black box: a post-hoc attribution method with its own assumptions, its own failure modes and no guarantee that the numbers it reports were ever computed by anything.


Leave the panels on shared scale for a moment before pressing the toggle. Carat swings the price by $8,103 across its range; table percentage swings it by $253. On a common axis, five of the nine curves are visibly almost flat, and that is the finding. Press own scale each and every panel fills its box with an interesting-looking wiggle. Both charts are honest. Only one of them tells you what matters, which is a good general warning about small multiples.


There is a catch, and the benchmark already showed it. Additivity is a real restriction, not a free lunch: the NAM lands at $1,070 against $526 for unrestricted boosting, so on this data readability costs about half the accuracy. Diamond prices interact. The value of an extra carat depends on the clarity it comes with, and no sum of one-feature curves can express that.


Which is why the most useful model on this page is probably the one that splits the difference. Explainable boosting machines [3] fit exactly the same additive form, but with boosted trees as the shape functions instead of neural networks, cycling through features one at a time with a small learning rate so that credit is shared rather than grabbed. Add a budget of explicit pairwise terms \(f_{jk}(x_j, x_k)\), each of which is still a surface you can plot, and the score goes from $1,002 to $588. That is within twelve percent of the black box, from a model where every component is a chart. If someone has to sign off on a decision, this is the trade to reach for first.

What TabNet Is Actually Doing

TabNet [4] is the best known attempt to give a network the thing trees have. Its argument is that a tree works by selecting one feature at a time, in sequence, with each choice depending on what happened before, so a network should do the same: several decision steps, each attending to a subset of features, each seeing what the last step already used.


The mechanism that makes that more than a slogan is the normaliser. A softmax cannot switch anything off, because \(e^{z}\) is strictly positive: every feature always keeps some weight, however small, and "the model ignored this column" is never literally true. TabNet uses sparsemax [5] instead, which is defined as the Euclidean projection onto the simplex,

$$ \operatorname{sparsemax}(z) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{p \in \Delta} \lVert p - z \rVert^2 $$

and a projection lands on the boundary of a set whenever the point is outside it. On the simplex, the boundary is exactly where some coordinates are zero. Drag the slider: the feature scores below are real, taken from how far the additive model above moves the price across each feature's range.

score sharpness

At low sharpness the two normalisers agree, because nothing stands far enough from the pack for the projection to leave the interior. Push it up and sparsemax starts dropping features outright while softmax merely makes them small. The closed form is a sort and a subtraction: find how many of the top scores survive, subtract the same \(\tau\) from each, clip the rest to zero. It is differentiable almost everywhere, which is the only property that actually mattered for putting it in a network.


This is a genuinely good idea, and it is worth separating from the question of whether TabNet is worth running. On this dataset it is not: $700 against $526, forty seconds against one, and a hundred-dollar spread across seeds. The published results that favour it tend to involve datasets far larger than 54,000 rows, and that is the honest boundary condition rather than a dismissal.

When The Network Is Right Anyway

Everything above is about accuracy on a fixed table, which is the question benchmarks ask and often not the question you have. There are four situations where a network is the correct choice even knowing all of this, and none of them are about beating boosting on RMSE.


The table is not the only input. A listing with a price, a description and a photograph is not a tabular problem. A network can consume all three and learn a shared representation; boosting cannot, and gluing a separate image model onto it means hand-designing the interface between them. This is the most common good reason by a distance.


A column has enormous cardinality. Half a million product IDs, or user IDs, or postcodes. Target encoding works and is what CatBoost does well, but a learned embedding puts similar entities near each other in a space you can then reuse, which target encoding does not.


You need to transfer or to keep learning. A boosted ensemble is fitted once against one target. A network can be pre-trained on unlabelled rows, fine-tuned for a new task, or updated on new data without refitting from scratch. Where labels are scarce and raw rows are abundant, that asymmetry is worth real accuracy.


The output is structured. Multi-task heads, sequences, embeddings consumed by something downstream, or a differentiable loss that expresses a business rule rather than an error. Boosting fits one scalar at a time.


Notice the shape of that list. Every entry is about what surrounds the model, not about the model's fit. On the narrow question of predicting one number from a fixed set of columns, the evidence on this page points one way and has for a decade.

The Verdict

Fit gradient boosting first. It beat the best neural model here by $134 and the best of everything else by $62, and it did it twenty times faster than anything else that came close. That combination is what actually matters: the cheap model is the one you get to tune, and tuning is where the remaining accuracy lives.


When somebody has to be able to read the model, reach for an explainable boosting machine with pairwise terms before reaching for a post-hoc explanation of a black box. Twelve percent of accuracy is a real price, but it buys a model whose every component is a chart rather than an approximation of one.


Reach for a network when something in the problem is not a table: another modality, a column with a million levels, a pre-training signal, a structured output. Not because you expect it to fit better. It probably will not.


And treat the two experiments at the top of this page as the reason rather than as trivia. Rotation invariance and robustness to junk columns are properties, not accidents, and knowing which properties a model family has is what lets you predict where it will do well before you have run anything. That is worth more than any single benchmark.

Six articles, and the arc through them is a steady surrender of assumptions: a straight line, then a line with its coefficients pulled toward zero, then a line that stops trusting the majority of its own data, then no line at all but a weighted vote among neighbours, then a long sum of yes-or-no questions, and finally here, a function assembled by gradient descent with no shape assumed anywhere. The last of those is the most flexible and, on this data, not the best. That is a fitting note to end a module on.
