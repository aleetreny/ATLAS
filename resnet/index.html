Depth

Fifty-six layers that fit their own training data worse than fourteen, and the one line that fixed it

aleetreny

Module 1.3 · Computer Vision · Architecture

The previous article handed the choice of features to the data and kept one decision for the human: the shape of the network that does the learning. Between 2012 and 2015 that decision was where the entire field's effort went, and the most obvious knob on it is the one this article turns. How many layers?


The first good answer, VGG in 2014, was almost aggressively boring: use 3 × 3 convolutions everywhere, never anything else, and just stack more of them. The second answer arrived because the first one stopped working, in a way that surprised the people it happened to. Beyond a certain depth, adding layers made networks worse at fitting the data they had already been shown. Not worse at generalising. Worse at memorising, which is supposed to be the easy part.


Everything below is measured on Fashion-MNIST rather than handwritten digits, and the choice matters: this article is about optimisation failing, and it needs a task where optimisation is the bottleneck. Twelve networks were trained for the ladder, from 8 layers to 56, in two families that differ by one addition. Here is the task: ten classes of clothing at 28 pixels square, where a pullover, a coat and a shirt are genuinely difficult to tell apart.

Why Everything Became 3 × 3

A convolution sees a small window. Stack two of them and the second one sees a window of windows: each layer adds \(k-1\) to the reach of the one below it, so \(L\) layers of \(k \times k\) end up looking at

$$ 1 + L\,(k - 1) $$

pixels across. Two 3 × 3 layers therefore see the same 5 × 5 window as a single 5 × 5 layer, and three of them see the same 7 × 7. VGG's argument was that you should always prefer the stack, because it is cheaper and because it puts a nonlinearity between the steps. Drag the sliders and watch the window grow.

kernel: 3×3
layers stacked: 2

Now press channels double here and try the 5 × 5 window again, because that second button is where the usual telling of this argument quietly cheats. The saving of \(2 \cdot 9C^2\) against \(25C^2\) assumes the layer keeps the same number of channels. Real networks double the channels wherever they halve the resolution, and in such a stage only the first layer of a stack is cheap: the rest work at the wider count. The arithmetic becomes \(18C^2 + 36(L-1)C^2\) against \(2k^2C^2\), and it does not favour stacking until the window reaches 7.


That is a claim about arithmetic, so here it is against a training run. Four networks, three stages each, the same channels, the same pooling, the same seed: the only thing that changes is how each stage's window is built.

Each stage Window Weights in the convolutions Training Test
one 5 × 5 5 144,600 90.47% 87.05%
two 3 × 3 5 160,920 (11% more) 93.95% 88.13%
one 7 × 7 7 283,416 92.28% 87.45%
three 3 × 3 7 269,784 (5% less) 94.95% 89.10%

Stacking wins both comparisons, by 1.08 points of test accuracy at the 5 × 5 window and 1.65 points at the 7 × 7. But notice where the parameter saving is: at the 5 × 5 window the stack costs more, not less, and only at 7 does it start paying for itself, exactly where the arithmetic above says the crossover is. So the popular version of VGG's argument, that small kernels are cheaper, is the half that is conditional. The half that held everywhere we measured is the other one: an extra nonlinearity between two small convolutions is worth more than the same window seen in one go.

The Ladder, Both Ways

Here is the whole ladder at once. The button matters more than it looks: on the test set a deeper network doing worse is unremarkable, and everybody has a word for it. The same shape on the training set is the finding, because a network that cannot fit the data it was shown is not overfitting. It is failing to optimise, and no amount of extra data would help.

The Obvious Explanation, Tested

Ask why a deep network trains badly and you will be told the gradients vanish: each layer multiplies the signal on its way back, and after fifty multiplications there is nothing left. It is a real phenomenon, it has a standard fix, and that fix, batch normalisation, is present in every network on this page, plain and residual alike. That is not an oversight. It is the control: the degradation has to be measured in a setting where the usual culprit has already been dealt with, or the article is just repeating folklore.


So measure it. Below are the gradient norms arriving at every convolution at initialisation, before a single step has been taken, which is when the vanishing-gradient hypothesis makes its prediction.

Which leaves the uncomfortable version of the problem. The deep network is not starved of signal and it is not overfitting. It is being asked to find a setting of its weights that we know exists, and it is not finding it. That is a statement about the search, not about the network's capacity, and it is the reason the fix is a change to what the layers are asked to compute rather than a change to how they are trained.

Change Nothing, Cheaply

Here is the argument that produced the fix, and it is almost annoyingly simple. Take a network that works, and add layers to the end of it. If those extra layers all computed the identity, passing their input through untouched, the deeper network would score exactly what the shallower one scored. A deeper network can therefore never be forced to be worse. It has a solution available by construction.


It just cannot find it. Making a stack of convolutions reproduce its own input is a hard thing to learn, and gradient descent does not stumble on it. So change what the layer computes. Instead of asking a block for \(H(x)\) directly, wire its input around it and ask only for the difference:

$$ H(x) \;=\; F(x) + x $$

Now "change nothing" means \(F(x) = 0\), which is the easiest thing a layer with weight decay can possibly do: drive the weights towards zero and the block steps aside. The identity stopped being something to learn and became the default.

Whichever way that came out, it is worth saying what the pair of measurements together are good for. One activation function beating another on a two-layer network tells you almost nothing about a fifty-six layer one, and the reverse is equally true. An architectural ingredient is not good or bad. It is good or bad at a scale, and the only way to know which is to run it at the scale you care about.
