Ten thousand questions per photograph, answered in milliseconds: the detector that put a face box in every camera

aleetreny

Module 1.3 · Computer Vision · Detection

The previous article asked what is in an image. This one asks a harder question: where. Nobody hands a detector a neat crop, so it must consider every place a face could be, at every size, and the arithmetic gets away from you immediately. The small photograph below is 128 pixels square, and even at a single fixed size it contains 10,816 possible 25-pixel windows. A video frame at 30 per second contains millions.


In 2001 Paul Viola and Michael Jones published a detector that did this in real time on a 700 MHz desktop, and it worked so well that it shipped in point-and-shoot cameras for a decade. It has three ideas in it, and every one of them is about cost: features cheap enough to be worth evaluating, an arithmetic trick that makes a big feature cost the same as a small one, and an arrangement that spends almost nothing on the overwhelming majority of windows, because the overwhelming majority of windows are obviously not faces.


All three are rebuilt here. The classifier on this page was trained from scratch, by AdaBoost over decision stumps, on 100 face crops and 2,000 background patches mined from ten face-free photographs, and the cascade it produced runs live in your browser over every window of a real picture. The counts you will see are not illustrations; the page computes them while you watch.

Where Is the Face?

Start by feeling the size of the problem.

A Photograph

No labels, no crops, no help. Somewhere in this picture there may be a face, and a detector has to decide not only whether but where, without being told either.

One Window

The trick that makes detection tractable is to turn it back into classification: cut out a small square and ask the yes-or-no question this atlas has been answering since module 1.2. Is this patch a face?

Ten Thousand Windows

Now ask it everywhere. Each dot is one window position, and at one fixed size there are 10,816 of them. If answering costs 40 feature evaluations, that is 432,640 evaluations for a single small picture at a single scale, and a real detector has several scales to get through, thirty times a second.

The First Four Features

So do not answer properly. Answer cheaply, and only for long enough to throw the window away: the cascade's first stage costs four features and eliminates 43% of everything on the first pass. Those windows are now finished forever.

What Survives

Eight stages later, 132 windows remain, and the average window in this picture cost 6.51 feature evaluations rather than 40. The boxes are not all faces, and the article is honest about that at the end. The economy is the point, and it is exact.

Any Rectangle, Four Lookups

The features are differences of rectangle sums: how much brighter is this band than the one below it. Adding up a rectangle costs one operation per pixel, which for a face-sized rectangle is hundreds, and there are tens of thousands of candidate features. That is the wall the whole method is built to walk around.


The way around it is one preprocessing pass. Build a table where each entry holds the sum of everything above and to the left of it, the integral image. After that, the sum over any rectangle at all is

$$ \text{sum} \;=\; D - B - C + A $$

four lookups at its corners, no matter how large it is. Drag a rectangle over the face below and watch the left counter grow with the area while the right one stays stubbornly at four. The table on the right lights up exactly the four cells being read.

Which Features Get Picked

At 25 by 25 pixels there are 166,400 possible two- and three-rectangle features, and this page's classifier was trained on a seeded random draw of 12,000 of them. AdaBoost, from the boosting article, does the choosing: at each round it takes the single feature and threshold that best fixes the mistakes the previous ones made, then reweights the training crops so the next round attends to what is still wrong.


The first feature it picked is worth a long look. It is a pair of wide horizontal bands across the middle of the face, and it fires when the lower band is brighter than the upper one: the eye region is darker than the cheekbones below it. That is, essentially, the feature on the first page of the original paper. Nobody told the algorithm about eyes; it searched twelve thousand rectangle patterns and this is what won.


Look at the two histograms, computed in your browser from the 200 crops in the sprite: they overlap. On its own, that best-in-twelve-thousand feature gets 88.6% of the held-out crops right and lets 12% of the background through. A single Haar feature is not a face detector. It is a cheap filter that is usually right, and the rest of the method is about what to do with a pile of those.

Stacking them works, and the measured gain is where you would expect it: not in accuracy, which starts high, but in false alarms. One feature passes 12.1% of background windows. Forty features pass 0.5%, a twenty-four-fold reduction, while detection goes from 95.5% to 97.7%. In detection the false-alarm rate is the number that matters, because there are so many more windows than faces: at 12% a small photograph produces a thousand false boxes.

The Cascade

Forty features on ten thousand windows is still 432,640 evaluations, and the third idea removes almost all of it. Nearly every window is obviously not a face, so do not spend forty features proving it. Run a few, and if the window fails, stop. Only the survivors go on to the next test, and only the survivors of that go further.


The stages here were grown the way the paper describes: keep adding features to the current stage until it keeps 98% of the faces while passing at most half the background still standing, then start a new one. That produced eight stages of 4, 1, 1, 3, 19, 1, 2 and 9 features. One detail matters for honesty: the thresholds were set on a held-out calibration split, never on the data the detector is judged on, for exactly the reason the calibration article spent a page on. A threshold is a fitted quantity too.

stages applied: none yet

Two measurements make the cascade's case, and one keeps it honest.


The economy: on the held-out crops the cascade spends 8.05 feature evaluations per window on average and 4.84 on the background ones, against 40 for running the whole ensemble everywhere. On the photograph, where background dominates as it does in real life, the average window costs 6.51 of 40, and the whole ten-thousand-window scan takes a few milliseconds in your browser. The consistency: the cascade returns a strict subset of what the full ensemble would have accepted, 132 of its 276 positive windows, with zero windows accepted by the cascade that the ensemble would have rejected. It is an accelerator, not a different classifier.


And the cost, stated plainly: those 144 windows the cascade drops that the full ensemble would have kept are the price of early exits. On the held-out crops the cascade detects 40 of 44 faces where the monolithic ensemble detects 43, while halving the false positives. A little detection, traded for a sixfold speedup, is exactly the bargain Viola and Jones proposed, and here it is with numbers on it.

The Verdict

What survives from 2001, and what this page's own detector cannot do.

Idea What it buys Measured here Watch out for
Integral image Rectangle sums in constant time Four lookups against one per pixel, at any size, after a single pass over the image. It only helps features made of axis-aligned rectangles, which is why the features look like that.
Boosting as feature selection 40 useful features out of 12,000 False alarms fall from 12.1% to 0.5% as the ensemble grows. Training scans every candidate every round: the selection, not the detector, is the slow part.
The cascade Almost nothing spent on background 6.51 features per window instead of 40 on a real photograph, output a strict subset of the ensemble's. Early exits are irreversible: 4 of 44 faces lost here that the full ensemble would have kept.
This page's detector A demonstration, not a product 132 surviving windows on one photograph, most of them not faces. 100 face crops and 40 features. The original used thousands of faces and 38 stages of 6,000.

That last row deserves its own sentence, because it is the honest end of this story. Everything on this page is real: real features, real boosting, a real cascade, real counts. What it is not is a face detector you would ship, and the gap is not cleverness but scale, in both data and stages. It is also the last article in this atlas where a person decides what the features may look like. Rectangles were chosen because integral images make rectangles free, and the model was allowed to pick among them but never to invent one.


The next article in this branch hands that decision over: a model that starts from small random filters and adjusts them by gradient descent until they become whatever the data needed, which turns out to include an edge detector nobody asked for. That is where module 1.3 goes next, and where the hand-designed era ends.
