Module 2.1 · Clustering · Relaxations

Four articles in, this module has four answers to "what is a cluster": a territory around a mean, a bell with soft edges, a connected region of high density, and a branch of a tree. Every one of them arrived with fine print nobody read aloud.


K-means needs a mean, so it needs numbers you can average and a distance you can square. DBSCAN needs one radius for the whole dataset. All four need somebody to say how many, whether as k or as the height of a cut. This article takes away one clause at a time, with k-medoids, k-modes, OPTICS and mean shift, and measures what each removal costs, on the very same points: the blobs of seed 19, the failure gallery that broke k-means, the varying-density trap that broke DBSCAN, and the 178 bottles of wine.


All four run in this page. PAM, k-modes, the OPTICS ordering and mean shift are written out in the browser's own JavaScript and checked at load time against a reference implementation each: the Rust FasterPAM, the kmodes package, and scikit-learn twice over. Where the page and the reference disagree, the console says so. As it stands: the numbers below say how far apart they are.

Watch It Swap

Twenty-five points from the blobs you already know, and the oldest medoid algorithm there is: PAM, partitioning around medoids, from 1990. It has two phases and no randomness at all.

The Centre Has To Be One Of Us

One rule change, and it is the whole article: the centre of a cluster must be a point from the dataset. Not an average of the cluster, an actual member of it. That makes the search finite and, as it turns out, awkward: there are only so many sets of three points, but there are a lot of them.

Build, One

The first medoid is the point with the smallest total distance to everybody else. The spokes drawn from it are the quantity being minimised: not squared distance any more, just distance, added up.

Build, The Rest

Each further medoid is whichever point buys the largest total reduction, given the ones chosen so far. Greedy, cheap, and good enough to start from. Notice that every centre sits on a dot, because it is one.

Swap

Now the part that gives the algorithm its name. Consider every exchange of a medoid for a non-medoid, all of them, take the one that lowers the total most, and repeat until none does. This is hill climbing with an enormous neighbourhood, and it is why PAM is slow.

Settled

The rings are medoids: real points, the kind you can look up in the table the data came from. The diamonds are k-means' centres on the same points, and they float wherever the arithmetic left them. On clean data the two answers are nearly the same, which is the honest starting position for everything that follows.

Two Objectives, One Square

Written down, the change is one exponent and one constraint. K-means minimises squared distance to a free centre:

$$J=\sum_{i} \min_{c} \lVert x_i - \mu_c \rVert^2, \qquad \mu_c \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

and k-medoids minimises plain distance to a centre that has to be one of the points:

$$L=\sum_{i} \min_{c} d(x_i, m_c), \qquad m_c \in \{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$$

Two consequences fall straight out. First, d can be anything at all: no averaging happens anywhere in the algorithm, so a table of dissimilarities is enough, and it need not come from coordinates. Second, one far-away point contributes d to the total instead of d², and that single missing square is the entire robustness story, with a break-even distance you can compute before you run anything.


On the blobs, both algorithms agree.

Any Distance You Like

The second consequence is the one that gets k-medoids used in practice. Since nothing is ever averaged, the algorithm never touches the coordinates: hand it a table of dissimilarities and it works, whether that table came from euclidean distance, from an edit distance between strings, from how often two customers bought the same thing, or from a questionnaire nobody knows how to average. When There Is No Mean At All

Medoids drop the average as a convenience. Categorical data drops it as a fact: there is no mean of red, red, blue. The standard repair is k-modes, which changes two things and nothing else. Distance becomes the number of columns where two rows differ, the Hamming distance, and the centre of a cluster becomes the most common category in each column, its mode.


To measure it against something the module already knows, the same 178 bottles are binned: each of the 13 measurements is replaced by which third of the bottles it falls in, low, middle or high, so every column now holds one of three categories . That is a real loss of information and a real dataset, the kind a questionnaire produces. The question is what to do with it.

Every Radius At Once

The density article ended with a dataset that defeats DBSCAN completely: two tight clusters a bridgeable gap apart, and one diffuse cluster far away. Any radius short enough to keep the tight pair separate shatters the diffuse one; any radius long enough to hold the diffuse one together fuses the pair. HDBSCAN got out of it by building a hierarchy and flattening it. OPTICS, from 1999, gets out of it by refusing to answer the question at all.


It walks the dataset in a greedy order, always stepping to whichever unvisited point is currently cheapest to reach, and records for each point the density at which it became reachable:

$$r(p) = \max\big(\text{core}_k(o),\; d(o,p)\big)$$

where core is the distance to a point's k-th nearest neighbour, the same quantity DBSCAN uses to decide whether a point is dense enough to grow from. Plot those in walk order and the valleys are the clusters. The output is not a clustering. It is a one-dimensional picture of the density structure at every radius simultaneously, and a horizontal line across it is not a metaphor for DBSCAN's radius: it is exactly DBSCAN's radius, giving exactly DBSCAN's answer.

Nobody Says K

The last clause to go is the one every article in this module has leaned on. Mean shift never asks for k. Put a window of radius h around a point, move the point to the mean of whatever is inside, repeat. Every point climbs the density like water running backwards uphill, points that arrive at the same summit are one cluster, and the number of clusters is however many summits there turned out to be.


The shaded background below is the density being climbed, and it is not a smooth kernel estimate drawn for decoration: it is the flat count of points inside a window of the current bandwidth, which is the exact quantity the trajectories are following. Move the bandwidth and watch the number of summits change without anybody being asked.

bandwidth 2.00

Not having to say k is not the same as not having to say anything.


And then there is the bill.

The Verdict

Four relaxations, four prices, all of them measured on the datasets this module has been carrying since its first article.

Reach for When What it costs Watch out for
K-medoids Outliers you cannot remove, or a distance you cannot average Measured on the blobs. The whole distance matrix, so memory grows as n², and the search is a local one.
K-modes Categorical columns whose numbering is arbitrary Measured on the binned wine. It is not the most accurate option here. One-hot plus k-means is, and it is invariant too.
OPTICS Densities that differ across the dataset Measured on the trap. Its xi is a parameter like any other, and its default is worse than a tuned radius almost everywhere.
Mean shift k genuinely unknown, and clusters that are blobs Measured on the blobs and the rings. The bandwidth is a density scale, which is the parameter OPTICS just took away. Nothing is free.
k-means anyway Clean numeric data, k known, n large Measured on all of them. Four of the five datasets below were built to break it, which is why it loses that table. Read it that way.

That is five articles and ten algorithms answering one question, and the pattern across them is not that the clever ones win. It is that every assumption is doing work: take one away and you get something back, pay for it somewhere else, and the somewhere else is measurable before you commit. The last two chips of this module are the two that drop the final assumption of all, that an algorithm gets to look at the coordinates: affinity propagation and spectral clustering keep only the table of how similar each pair is, which is what finally solves the rings. After that the sideways pull takes over, because everything here has assumed the structure worth finding is groups. Sometimes it is directions, the few combinations of features along which the data actually varies, and that is where module 2.2 opens, on these same 178 bottles, which turn out to cluster slightly better once eleven of their thirteen dimensions have been thrown away.
