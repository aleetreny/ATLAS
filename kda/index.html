Kernel Discriminants

The last light of module 1: prove the linear ceiling with your own hands, then give Fisher's projection the kernel it was owed

aleetreny

Module 1.2 · Classification · Kernel discriminants

One chip in module 1 is still dark. The geometric classification article taught discriminant analysis as a projection: LDA hunts the single direction along which two classes, squashed to one dimension, separate best. It also taught the kernel trick, and then gave it to the SVM alone. Discriminant analysis never got its upgrade. This short article pays that debt and, with it, closes the module.


The demonstration data is a reunion: the two moons the DBSCAN article clustered without labels, now with their labels restored, 210 training points and 90 held out. Moons are the canonical linear-projection killer, and this article does not ask you to take that on authority. It hands you the projection direction as a dial, lets you try every angle yourself, and tells you the measured ceiling you are chasing: over a sweep of 721 directions, the best any straight projection scores on the held-out moons is 0.878. LDA, to its credit, finds that exact ceiling on its own. It simply cannot go higher, because no line can.


Then Fisher's same objective, between-class distance over within-class spread, is taken in the kernel's feature space instead, and the ceiling stops applying: 0.989 held-out, from the same projection idea. Everything on this page runs from the fitted dual coefficients live in your browser, boundaries and histograms alike, and the accuracies are re-derived at load time as a check.

A Projection Can Only Do So Much

The story in five steps: what a direction is, what the best one achieves, and what changes when the direction lives somewhere bigger.

The Moons, Labelled

Two interleaved crescents. The density article grouped them with no labels at all; today the labels are the point, because the question is not "are there groups" but "can a projection tell them apart".

What LDA Does

LDA picks one direction (the dark line), drops every point's shadow onto it, and hopes the two colours land apart. The histogram below the scatter is those shadows, and the pile-up in the middle is the interleaving doing its damage: held-out accuracy 0.878.

The Ceiling Is Measured

Maybe LDA chose badly? No: this is the best of 721 swept directions, and it scores the same 0.878. The failure is not the choice of angle. It is the concept of angle: every straight shadow folds part of each moon onto the other. In the next widget, the dial is yours.

Fisher, Kernelised

Keep the objective, change the space: separate the class means relative to the class spreads, but for a projection direction living in the kernel's feature space, expressed through weights on the training points. The shadows land in two clean islands, and the pile-up is gone.

Back in Two Dimensions

Seen from the original plane, the kernel direction is a curved boundary hugging both crescents: held-out accuracy 0.989 against the linear 0.878. One idea, one upgrade, eleven points of accuracy that no rotation of a line could ever buy.

Beat 0.878 If You Can

The projection direction is now yours. Every position of the slider projects all 210 training points onto that angle, grants the projection the most charitable threshold that exists (the dashed cut), and scores the 90 held-out points. The widget keeps your personal best. It also knows, from the sweep, that the record is 0.878, and the interesting part of playing is feeling why: whichever way you turn, the tips of one crescent cross the other's shadow before the bodies separate.

projection angle = 0 degrees

Fisher's Ratio, Then the Upgrade

Everything here maximises one ratio: how far apart the projected class means land, against how much the projected classes spread,

$$ J(w) \;=\; \frac{\big(w^{\top} m_1 - w^{\top} m_0\big)^2} {\,w^{\top} S_W\, w\,} $$

The kernel version replaces \(w\) with a direction in feature space written as a weighted sum over training points, \(w = \sum_i \alpha_i\, \phi(x_i)\), after which every quantity in the ratio becomes an expression in the kernel matrix and the whole problem collapses, exactly as ridge did last article, to one regularised linear solve for \(\alpha\). Same representer logic, third appearance. What the solution buys, and what it costs at the extremes, is one dial away.

γ = 0.3

The dial's two ends are the article's fine print. At \(\gamma = 0.01\) the kernel bumps are wider than the whole dataset and KDA collapses onto the linear ceiling to the decimal: 0.878. Every kernel machine carries its linear ancestor inside it. At \(\gamma = 1000\) the bumps are narrower than the gaps between points, training accuracy is a perfect 1.000, and the held-out score has fallen to 0.711: the overfitting cliff of article 8, re-enacted by a discriminant. The plateau between 0.1 and 30, two orders of magnitude wide, is why the middle of this dial feels so safe.

The Verdict, and the Module's Last Audit

On the moons the kernel is worth eleven points. On real tabular data, the module's closing audit repeats article 8's sober finding. Five-fold on the 569 biopsies: LDA 0.956, QDA 0.967, KDA (with its \(\gamma\) chosen by inner cross-validation) 0.967, the RBF support vector machine 0.981. The kernel discriminant buys the same two points QDA already had, and the SVM, which spends its kernel on a margin rather than on scatter matrices, keeps the crown it won nine articles ago.

SVM rbf 0.9807 ± 0.0103 Accuracy is the product: the margin spends the same kernel better on this data. No projection, no picture: you trade Fisher's interpretability for the margin's score.

The honest through-line of the whole module fits in one sentence: the powerful ideas were few (loss functions, penalties, kernels, margins, probabilities, trees), and most named methods are those few ideas recombined, which is exactly why every article measured its subject against relatives instead of taking its name at face value.
