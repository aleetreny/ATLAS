Regression without an equation: neighbours, kernels and Gaussian processes

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression

Every model so far has started with an act of faith. Least squares assumed the relationship was a straight line. Polynomial regression assumed it was a curve of a particular degree. GLMs assumed a distribution. Each time, we wrote down a shape first and let the data pick its parameters afterwards. That is what the word parametric means: the model has a fixed number of knobs, decided before you look.


This article does the opposite. Suppose you refuse to name a shape at all. To predict the fuel efficiency of a 150 horsepower car, you do not solve for a slope. You find the cars in your records with roughly 150 horsepower, and you report what happened to them.


That is the entire idea, and it sounds too simple to need a name. It has several, because the details of which neighbours you consult and how much each one counts turn out to matter enormously, and because the price for assuming nothing is steep and specific. We will meet k-nearest neighbours, kernel smoothers, local linear regression and Gaussian processes, in that order, and finish with the reason none of them saved us from deep learning.

The Parametric Bargain

A straight line commits to a claim about the entire range before seeing any particular car: that the relationship is constant everywhere. All 392 observations get compressed into two numbers. Efficient, interpretable, and here demonstrably wrong.

A Local Question

Instead, take one query, 190 horsepower, and ask only the cars near it. The dashed band holds its 15 nearest neighbours. Everything outside it is ignored completely. Average their efficiency, and that is the prediction. Then repeat for every value of x.

Too Few Neighbours

With k = 1, every prediction is whichever single car happens to be closest, so the curve chases every wiggle in the training set. Textbooks add that it therefore passes through every training point exactly. This one cannot, and the reason is worth a paragraph of its own below.

Too Many

With k = 120, every query consults roughly a third of the dataset. Neighbouring queries return nearly identical answers and the curve flattens towards the overall average. The model has stopped being local at all.

The Shape Nobody Specified

At k = 15 the curve bends where the data bends and flattens where the data flattens. Notice what did not happen: nobody wrote down a quadratic, or a log, or an exponential. The shape is a consequence of the data, not an assumption about it.

One Knob, Two Failures

k is not a nuisance parameter. It is the model. Small k means high variance: each prediction rests on few opinions, so it swings with the noise. Large k means high bias: the neighbourhood is so wide that genuinely different regions get averaged together. There is no third failure mode and no way to escape the trade, only a place to stand in the middle of it.


There is also a second choice hiding here. Standard k-nearest neighbours gives every one of the k an equal vote, which is strange: a car 2 horsepower away and one 40 horsepower away are not equally informative. Weighting each neighbour by \(1/d\) fixes that, and lets you use a larger, steadier k without blurring away local detail.

$$ \hat{y}(x_q) = \frac{\sum_{i \in N_k} w_i\, y_i}{\sum_{i \in N_k} w_i}, \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{d(x_q, x_i)} $$

Solid dots are training cars; hollow rings are the held-out quarter. Drag k from one extreme to the other and watch both scores.

k = 15

At k = 1 every car is its own nearest neighbour, so the training error should be exactly zero. Drag the slider there and read it: 4.770. The 294 training cars carry only 84 distinct horsepower readings between them, and twelve sit at 67 hp with mileages from 26.0 to 44.6. No function of horsepower can be right about all twelve, and the best any 1-nearest neighbour could manage here is 3.398. Memorisation is not always available. Overfitting is.

The Trouble at the Edges

k-nearest neighbours has an abrupt quality: a car is either one of the k or it is nothing. Kernel smoothing replaces that hard boundary with a continuous weighting. Every point contributes, with an influence that decays as it gets further away:

$$ K_h(d) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2}{2h^2}\right) $$

The bandwidth \(h\) now plays the role k did. Take a kernel-weighted average of the neighbours and you have the Nadaraya-Watson estimator, the smooth cousin of k-nearest neighbours:

$$ \hat{y}(x_q) = \frac{\sum_i K_h(x_i - x_q)\, y_i}{\sum_i K_h(x_i - x_q)} $$


And now a genuine flaw, one that is easy to state and easier to miss. A weighted average is a locally constant model: it answers every query with a number, implicitly assuming the truth is flat inside the window. In the middle of the data that is harmless, because the neighbours on the left and the right cancel out. At the edge of the data it is not, because there are no neighbours on one side. Every point in the window lies to the same side of the query, and their average is pulled inward. The curve flattens exactly where the trend is steepest.


Local linear regression repairs this by fitting a short straight line inside each window instead of a single level, using distances measured from the query itself:

$$ \min_{a,\,b} \; \sum_i K_h(x_i - x_q)\,\bigl(y_i - a - b\,(x_i - x_q)\bigr)^2 $$

The prediction is \(a\), the height of that local line at the query. Because the line is free to tilt, a one-sided neighbourhood is no longer a problem: it extrapolates the local trend instead of averaging it away.


Below, both estimators on the same data. The shaded strips are the outer 10% at each end. Move the query marker into one of them, then widen the window, and watch the orange curve bend towards the middle while the green one holds its line.

window h = 46 query at 110 hp

The numbers are stark. Widen the window to h = 46, a quarter of the horsepower range, and the kernel average is roughly 84% worse at the edges than local linear regression, while in the middle 80% of the range the two are close. That is the signature of boundary bias: not a general loss of accuracy, but a specific failure in a specific place, caused by a specific assumption.


Two footnotes worth carrying. First, local linear regression is what LOESS does, so if you have ever added a smooth trend line to a scatter plot, you have used this. Second, notice that k-NN is really a kernel smoother in disguise: a box-shaped kernel whose width adapts to keep the number of neighbours constant. All of these are the same idea wearing different clothes.

A Model That Admits Doubt

Every method so far shares a flaw so basic it is easy to overlook: they answer with equal confidence everywhere. Ask a k-nearest neighbours model about a region where you have no data at all and it will find the k closest points, however far away, and hand you a number with a straight face.


A Gaussian process refuses to do that. Rather than fitting a function, it maintains a probability distribution over functions. The prior is the vague belief that the truth is some smooth curve; observing data conditions that distribution, squeezing it tight where you have measurements and leaving it loose where you do not. What comes back is not a prediction but a mean and a variance at every point.


The entire modelling decision lives in the kernel, which states how strongly two points should be correlated as a function of their separation. RBF says the world is very smooth. Matérn says it is rougher and more jagged. Periodic says it repeats. The kernel is not a technicality: it is your assumption about reality, written down honestly for once.


Click on the chart to place observations. The dashed line is the truth being sampled, hidden from the model. Watch what the shaded band does where you add data, and what it does where you do not.


Two things about that band are worth stating precisely, because they are what people get wrong about Gaussian processes. The dark band is one posterior standard deviation and the pale one is two, roughly 68% and 95%. It is a credible interval, not a confidence interval: the Bayesian statement that the curve lies inside it with that probability, given the prior and the data. And it covers the underlying curve, not the next noisy measurement, which is why at an observation it narrows to the noise level rather than to zero. A model that claimed zero uncertainty where it had seen one noisy reading would be claiming the reading was exact.

length scale 1.20

This is worth more than it first appears. In a self-driving car or a medical decision system, a model that can say I have never seen anything like this is categorically more useful than one that cannot, because that signal is what triggers a handover to a human. It is also the engine of Bayesian optimisation: when each experiment is expensive, the variance surface tells you where the next one will teach you the most.


Now the same idea over a map. Four hundred California districts, each with a median house value, and a Gaussian process fitted on longitude and latitude alone. One button shows what it predicts, from $76k to $431k, the other shows how much it trusts itself. The second view is the interesting one, and not for the reason you would expect.

One honest caveat about that map. Distances here are computed on raw longitude and latitude after standardising each axis, which is a convenience, not a truth: at California's latitude a degree of longitude is about 88 km against 111 km for a degree of latitude. A serious spatial model would use great-circle distance. It is worth noticing that a method advertised as assumption-free still smuggled in an assumption, and it was hiding inside the word "distance".

Why This Does Not Scale

Everything on this page rests on one word: nearby. In one dimension that word is solid. Add features, and it quietly stops meaning anything at all.


The arithmetic is brutal and exact. Spread data uniformly through a unit cube in \(d\) dimensions. To capture a fraction \(r\) of it, a neighbourhood must have edge length \(r^{1/d}\) along every axis. In one dimension, the closest 10% of your data lies within 10% of the range. With ten features, that same 10% requires a box covering 79% of every axis. Your local neighbourhood is now almost the entire dataset, and "local" has become a word with no content.


The second curve says the same thing from the other side. It plots the distance to the nearest point divided by the distance to a typical one. In one dimension the nearest neighbour is dramatically closer than average, so it carries real information. By a hundred features that ratio has climbed to 0.81: the closest point is nearly as far away as any other. There is no such thing as a neighbour any more.

This is why non-parametric methods rule in one, two and three dimensions and vanish above about ten, and why the next article is about a family that does not measure distance at all. There is a cost story too: k-nearest neighbours does no work at fit time and all of it at prediction time, scanning the training set for every query, and an exact Gaussian process needs to factorise an \(n \times n\) matrix, which is \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) work and \(\mathcal{O}(n^2)\) memory. At ten thousand points that is already uncomfortable. At a million it is out of the question without approximations.

The Verdict

Four ways to answer a question with the data itself, and one clear rule for when any of them is the right idea: few features, enough data, and a relationship you cannot write down.

Method Defines "nearby" as… Cost Reach for it when… Watch out for
k-NN A fixed count of closest points Nothing to fit, O(n) per query You want a baseline in an afternoon, on few features, with plenty of data. Discontinuous predictions; needs feature scaling; k is the whole model.
Nadaraya-Watson A fixed radius, weighted smoothly O(n) per query You want the same idea with a continuous, differentiable output. Flattens badly at the edges of the data. Rarely the right final answer.
Local linear (LOESS) Same, but fits a tilt not a level A tiny solve per query Almost always, over Nadaraya-Watson. It is the honest default smoother. Slightly more variance in sparse regions; still one or two features only.
Gaussian process A covariance function you choose O(n³) to fit, O(n²) memory You need calibrated uncertainty, or each observation is expensive. Cubic scaling; the kernel is a strong assumption wearing a neutral name.

The through-line of this module has been a slow retreat from assumption. Least squares assumed a line, polynomials assumed a degree, GLMs assumed a distribution, robust methods assumed most of the data was honest, and here we assumed only that similar inputs give similar outputs. That last assumption is the weakest of the lot, and it is still an assumption, and it still fails, in high dimensions, for reasons that have nothing to do with the amount of data you collected.


Two roads lead on from here. One keeps the kernels you just met and hands them a budget: SVR and Kernel Ridge, where the Gaussian process from this article turns out to be hiding inside a much cheaper machine, and the identity between them is verified to thirteen decimal places. The other abandons distance entirely and asks a different question, one yes-or-no question at a time, thousands of times over, and beats everything on this page on tabular data. That story is called Gradient Boosting.
