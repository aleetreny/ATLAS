Gradient Boosting

A committee of weak trees, each one fitted to the last one's mistakes

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression

Every model in this module so far has tried to be right in one go. Fit the line, fit the curve, fit the smoother, and whatever error remains is what you live with. Boosting takes a stranger route. It builds a model that is barely a model at all, looks at what that model got wrong, builds a second one to predict the mistakes, adds a small fraction of it, and then repeats several hundred times.


Nothing in that description sounds powerful. Each individual member of the committee is a stump: a single yes-or-no question, two possible answers. And yet on the kind of data that fills most spreadsheets, ensembles built this way have beaten everything else for more than a decade, including, stubbornly, neural networks.


The idea is due to Jerome Friedman [1], and his framing is the one worth carrying: each new tree is a step of gradient descent in function space. Ordinary gradient descent nudges numbers to reduce a loss. Boosting nudges the function itself, and the direction it nudges in is the residual.


Formally the ensemble after \(M\) rounds is a sum of small corrections, each scaled by a learning rate \(\nu\):

$$ F_M(x) = F_0(x) + \nu \sum_{m=1}^{M} h_m(x) $$

where \(F_0\) is a constant and each \(h_m\) is fitted to the residual left by everything before it. That is genuinely the whole algorithm. Let's watch it run.

Fit the Mistakes

Now fit one stump, not to the data, but to those residuals. One threshold, two constants. It cannot capture the curve, and it is not trying to. It only has to be slightly better than nothing at describing where the errors are positive and where they are negative.

Take a Small Step

Add that stump to the ensemble, but scaled down by the learning rate. Deliberately taking less than the full correction is what keeps the committee from lurching, and it is the single most important regularizer boosting has. The residuals shrink a little.

Round Five

Five stumps in. The fit is a coarse staircase, and the residual panel has visibly flattened in the regions those five splits covered. Every subsequent tree will be handed a harder, smaller problem than the one before it.

Round Twenty-Five

The staircase now has enough steps to follow the curve up and back down. Notice that no single tree here understands the shape. The shape is an emergent property of the sum.

Round One Hundred and Twenty

A smooth-looking curve made entirely of step functions, and residuals that are nearly structureless noise. This is what "gradient descent in function space" buys: an arbitrarily flexible model assembled out of parts that individually cannot bend at all.

Small Steps, Many of Them

The learning rate \(\nu\) and the number of trees \(M\) are not two independent settings. They are one setting seen from two directions, and the product of them is roughly how far the ensemble travels. Halve the rate and you need about twice the trees to arrive at the same place.


Given that, why not always take big steps and save the compute? Because the route matters. Small steps let later trees correct the overshoot of earlier ones, and the resulting ensemble generalises better at the same training error. This is Friedman's shrinkage, and it is why every practical default sits between 0.01 and 0.1.


The upper chart is a live fit on the toy curve. The lower chart is measured on 53,920 real diamonds: validation error against tree count, one line per learning rate. Notice the shape of the family.

Read the lower chart carefully, because it contains the practical rule. At \(\nu = 1\) the model gets somewhere fast and then gets worse as trees accumulate. At \(\nu = 0.01\) it is still improving after four hundred trees and has not arrived. Everything in between converges to roughly the same floor, and the slower rates converge to a slightly better one. Time is the only thing you are trading.

Knowing When to Stop

Boosting has no natural end. Every additional tree reduces the training error, forever, because there is always some residual left to fit. Left alone it will eventually fit the noise perfectly, which is why the number of trees is itself a regularization parameter and why you must measure when to stop rather than decide in advance.


Below, a deliberately aggressive configuration on the diamond data: rate 0.6, depth 6. The training curve falls without limit. The held-out curve does something else entirely.

stop after 16 trees

The held-out error bottoms out at tree 16 and then climbs steadily, while the training error keeps falling all the way to $133. By tree 400 the model predicts its own training stones almost perfectly and has given back roughly sixty dollars of accuracy on stones it has never seen. Every library implements early_stopping_rounds for exactly this reason: watch a validation set, and stop when it stops improving.


Notice also how early that optimum arrives here. That is the aggressive learning rate talking. At the gentler default of 0.1 this same model never turns around at all within any tree budget worth plotting, which is the clearest possible argument for shrinkage: a small learning rate is the cheapest overfitting insurance boosting sells.

What XGBoost Actually Changed

Friedman's algorithm fits each tree to the residual and then works out what each leaf should say by averaging. Tianqi Chen's XGBoost [2] replaced that with something sharper: expand the loss to second order around the current prediction, and the whole tree-building problem turns into arithmetic.


Write \(g_i\) for the gradient of the loss at sample \(i\) and \(h_i\) for its second derivative. For a leaf holding some set of samples, with \(G = \sum g_i\) and \(H = \sum h_i\), the output that minimises the regularized objective is not an average but a ratio:

$$ w^{*} = -\frac{G}{H + \lambda} $$

and the value of holding that leaf is \(\tfrac{1}{2} G^2/(H+\lambda)\). A split is then worth making only if the two children are together worth more than the parent, after paying a fixed toll \(\gamma\) for the extra leaf:

$$ \text{gain} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}\right] - \gamma $$

Three things fall out of this at once. Pruning stops being a heuristic and becomes a sign test on a number. \(\lambda\) shrinks leaf outputs, exactly as ridge regression shrinks coefficients in the regularization article. And \(\gamma\) puts an explicit price on tree complexity. Move the threshold below and watch all of it respond.

Two Ways to Grow a Tree

LightGBM [3] arrived from Microsoft Research with a different question: given a budget of splits, where should you spend them? Classic implementations grow level-wise, finishing an entire depth before descending, which keeps trees balanced and predictable. LightGBM grows leaf-wise, always splitting whichever leaf anywhere in the tree promises the largest loss reduction.


Leaf-wise reaches the profitable parts of the tree sooner, so it buys more improvement per split. It also produces deep, lopsided trees that will happily memorise a small dataset, which is why num_leaves is the parameter that matters most in LightGBM and why setting it too high is the classic way to ruin a model with it.


Same tree, same numbers, same budget. The only difference is the order the splits are spent in.

splits used: 4

LightGBM's other big idea was histogram binning: instead of considering every distinct value of a feature as a candidate split, bucket the values into a couple of hundred bins once, and search only the bin edges. It sounds like an approximation that should cost accuracy. On these diamonds it costs essentially nothing and saves an order of magnitude: at identical settings, exact search takes 8.7 s for RMSE $572.4 while the histogram version takes 0.8 s for $572.6. The idea was convincing enough that XGBoost adopted it, so since version 2.0 its default tree method is histogram-based too, and the old "XGBoost is exact, LightGBM is approximate" framing you will still find in blog posts is out of date.


The third library in the usual trio, CatBoost [4], attacks a subtler problem. In standard boosting the same rows are used both to compute the residuals and to fit the tree that corrects them, which leaks a little of each target into its own prediction and biases the ensemble. CatBoost's ordered boosting fixes this by fixing a random permutation and computing each row's residual using only a model trained on rows that came before it. It also handles raw categorical columns natively, by target statistics computed under that same ordering.

Does the Library Matter?

This is the question every introduction dances around, so here is a straight answer measured on 53,920 diamonds, three runs each. The catch in most published comparisons is that they run each library at its own defaults, which measures whose defaults are bolder rather than whose algorithm is better. Every boosted row below is pinned to the same capacity: 300 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.1.

Implementation Held-out RMSE Fit time Relative speed
scikit-learn, exact splits $572.4 ± 0.4 8.67 s 1×
scikit-learn, histogram $572.6 ± 0.5 0.76 s 11×
CatBoost $571.7 ± 0.5 0.73 s 12×
LightGBM $569.9 ± 0.0 0.29 s 30×
XGBoost $573.2 ± 0.0 0.17 s 51×

Read the accuracy column first, because it is the surprising one. Five different implementations, three of them the celebrated competition libraries, and they span $3.30 of RMSE, about half of one percent. At matched capacity they are, statistically, the same model. Which they should be: they are all fitting shallow trees to residuals, and the algorithm was published in 2001.


Now read the time column, where the same five implementations span a factor of 51. That is what the modern libraries actually sell. Not a better estimator, but the same estimator delivered in sub-second time through histogram binning, cache-aware data layout, multithreading and sparsity-aware split finding. On this dataset that turns an eight-second experiment into a fifth of a second, which changes how many experiments you are willing to run, which is how you actually get a better model.


And to make the point sharper: letting LightGBM use its own default capacity of 31 leaves instead of the depth-3 straitjacket takes RMSE from $569.9 to $516.6, in the same 0.3 seconds. That one setting is worth sixteen times more accuracy than the entire choice of library. Pick whichever you like, then go and tune it.


Measured on Windows AMD64 with xgboost 3.2.0, lightgbm 4.7.0, catboost 1.2.10 and scikit-learn 1.8.0, Python 3.11.7, three repeats per row, all cores available. Timings are wall clock and will move with thread count and hardware; the accuracy column will not.

How to Feed It a Category

Diamonds come with three text columns: cut, colour and clarity. Trees split on numbers, so something has to happen to those columns first, and the choice matters more than most people expect. Three options, same library, same settings, same split.

Treatment Columns Held-out RMSE What happened
Ordinal codes 9 $569.9 Fair → Ideal becomes 0 → 4. Best of the three, because these grades really are ordered.
Native categorical 9 $573.6 The library partitions category sets directly. No gain here, and no loss.
One-hot 26 $628.0 Seventeen extra columns and 10% worse. The clear loser.

One-hot encoding is the reflex most people learned first, and for tree ensembles it is usually the wrong reflex. Each binary column can only ever answer "is it this one category or not", so a split that would cleanly separate three grades from five has to be built out of several weak splits instead, and each of them fragments the data further. The cost here is ten percent of accuracy for seventeen extra columns.


The more interesting row is the middle one. Native categorical handling is the headline feature of both LightGBM and CatBoost, and here it does nothing at all. That is not a failure, it is a lesson about when the feature is for: cut, colour and clarity are ordered grades, so mapping them to 0 through 4 encodes real information, and a method that treats them as unordered sets can only match it. Native handling earns its reputation on genuinely unordered, high-cardinality columns, postcodes, product IDs, user agents, where an ordinal code would be meaningless and one-hot would explode.


CatBoost's ordered target statistics on these same columns came out at $596.6 and took eight times longer, for the same reason: machinery built to prevent target leakage across thousands of rare categories is overhead when you have eight ordered grades. Reach for the feature when the data calls for it, not because the documentation is proud of it.

Reading the Importances, Carefully

Boosted ensembles hand you a feature importance ranking for free, which is a large part of their appeal and a reliable source of confident nonsense. Here is the ranking from a model trained on all 53,920 diamonds.

A diamond's price is famously driven by carat. This model says the most important feature is y, the width in millimetres, and gives carat less than half as much credit. Both statements are true and neither is informative, because y correlates with carat at 0.95: they are two measurements of one physical quantity. When features are near-duplicates, gain-based importance has to divide the credit between them, and how it divides is close to arbitrary.


The lesson is not that importances are useless. It is that they measure what the model used, not what matters, and those two things come apart exactly when your columns are correlated, which for real data is most of the time. If you need attributions you can reason about, reach for SHAP values, which are built on a game-theoretic account of how to share credit fairly among cooperating features.


Twenty of the 53,940 stones list a width, depth or height of exactly zero, which is physically impossible and marks a recording error. They are dropped before anything on this page is fitted.

The Verdict

Everything measured on the same 53,920 diamonds, same split, three runs each. The comparison is not a leaderboard, it is a shape:

Model Held-out RMSE Fit time What it tells you
Ridge $1,200 0.00 s A straight line through a price surface that is anything but. The floor everything else is measured against.
One deep tree $625 0.09 s Half the error of the line, from a model that only asks yes-or-no questions. Structure beats smoothness here.
Random forest $515 1.6 s Averaging many independent deep trees. Note that it beat both boosted models at default settings.
Boosting, depth 3 $570 0.29 s 300 shallow trees in sequence, LightGBM. Slower to write and worse than the forest at this capacity.
Boosting, 31 leaves $517 0.30 s The same library given room to breathe. One parameter, $53 of accuracy, no extra time.

The two boosted rows are the lesson. At depth 3 the ensemble loses to a random forest that was not tuned at all; give the same library its default 31 leaves and it draws level, in three tenths of a second. Boosting's advantage was never that it wins out of the box. It is that it has far more room to be tuned, and it responds to tuning fast enough that you can afford to do it. A forest is the better untuned model; a boosted ensemble is the better tuned one.


And if you want boosting without a new dependency, scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingRegressor is the histogram-based implementation that appeared in the library table, within a dollar of XGBoost at matched settings. It is genuinely competitive, and it is already installed.
