SVR and Kernel Ridge

The unfinished business of regression: a tube that ignores small errors, a ridge gone nonlinear, and the Gaussian process hiding inside it

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression · Kernel machines

Module 1.1 has owed two debts since its own articles announced them. The regularization article taxed coefficients but only ever drew straight lines. The SVM article built the widest street and the kernel trick, then spent them entirely on classification. This article pays both debts on the dataset the whole branch grew up on: the 392 cars, horsepower against fuel economy, the same 294/98 split the non-parametric article measured its neighbours on.


The two machines answer one question two ways: what should an error cost? Every regression so far said "its square, from the first millimetre". Support vector regression answers with a tube: misses smaller than \(\varepsilon\) cost exactly nothing, and only the cars that touch or leave the tube, the support vectors, hold the curve up. The reward for institutionalised indifference is sparsity, and it is measured here: the best fit of the whole sweep keeps 125 cars of 294 on the payroll.


Kernel ridge keeps the squared loss and instead upgrades the geometry: article 2's ridge penalty, applied in the kernel feature space of article 8. No tube, no sparsity, every car on the books forever, and in exchange, a luxury nothing else in this module has: a closed form. The whole model is one linear solve, which is why on this page it refits live under your fingers, 294 equations per slider tick, checked against scikit-learn at load. And hiding inside that solve is an old friend from article 4, revealed at the end to thirteen decimal places.

The Tube

Third appearance of the banana. A straight line failed it in article 1 and neighbours traced it in article 4; now watch a tube learn which cars matter.

What an Error Costs

Every model in this branch so far paid squared loss on every residual: be off by half a mile per gallon and you owe a quarter. The straight line is what that buys on curved data. The question this article opens with: what if small errors simply did not matter?

Declare Them Free

Wrap the curve in a tube of half-width \(\varepsilon\) = 2 mpg. Any car inside pays nothing at all, exactly zero, and only cars outside pay, linearly, for the distance by which they escape. This is the \(\varepsilon\)-insensitive loss, and it changes who has power over the fit.

The Payroll

Because interior cars cost nothing, they exert nothing: the highlighted cars, the ones on or outside the tube wall, are the only points whose removal would move the curve. Everything grey is deadweight in the best sense: representable, deletable, free.

Squeeze It

Tighten the tube to half a mile per gallon and the free zone nearly vanishes: 252 of 294 cars are back on the payroll. The model has become a near-interpolator with extra steps, and the test error is no better for all that staffing.

The Sweet Spot Is Wide

At \(\varepsilon = 3\), C = 10, the sweep's best test error (4.800 mpg) comes from just 125 support vectors. Ignoring small errors is not an approximation to the right answer. On noisy data it is the right answer: the tube is a form of regularisation, and the dials below let you feel it.

Two Dials, One Payroll

The loss that produces all of this is a flat floor with two ramps:

$$ L_\varepsilon(r) \;=\; \max\!\big(0,\; |r| - \varepsilon\big) $$

\(\varepsilon\) sets the tube's width in mpg: how big a miss has to be before it exists. C prices the misses that do: article 8's slack budget, unchanged. Between them they trade error for sparsity, and the right panel shows the whole measured trade at once: the support-vector count collapsing from 252 to 21 as the tube widens from 0.5 to 8, while the test error dips at 3 and then pays for the emptiness. Every state is a real scikit-learn fit.

ε = 2 mpg C = 10

Kernel Ridge, Refit Under Your Fingers

The other machine keeps squared loss and upgrades the space instead. Ridge regression in the kernel's feature space collapses, through the same representer logic as the SVM, to one equation:

$$ \alpha \;=\; \big(K + \lambda I\big)^{-1} y, \qquad \hat{f}(x) \;=\; \sum_{i} \alpha_i\, k(x, x_i) $$

No margin, no tube, no quadratic program: one symmetric linear system, 294 equations for 294 cars, every car a coefficient forever. The closed form is not a footnote, it is the product, and this widget proves it the only honest way: every tick of these sliders rebuilds the kernel matrix and re-solves the system in your browser, in about a millisecond, with the result checked against twelve scikit-learn reference fits at load. Push \(\gamma\) high and watch the curve start memorising individual cars; push \(\lambda\) high and watch article 2's tax flatten it toward the prior.

γ = 0.5 λ = 0.1

The Twins

One reveal to close the machinery. Article 4's Gaussian process produced its elegant posterior mean from priors, likelihoods and marginalisation. Take that GP, same RBF kernel, same noise level \(\lambda\) on the diagonal, zero-mean prior, and compare its posterior mean with the kernel ridge fit at the same settings: the curves agree to less than 10-12 mpg everywhere, because they are the same linear system solved under two vocabularies. Bayes bought the error bands and the principled hyperparameters; algebra bought the same curve for one solve. Knowing that the two dialects name one object is worth more than either dialect.

The Verdict

Measured on all six numeric features, five-fold, against the branch's own baseline: ridge 3.440 mpg of error, kernel ridge 2.651, SVR 2.695 with roughly 197 support vectors per fold. On these cars the kernel genuinely pays (the relationship really is curved, as article 1's polynomial first showed), and the two kernel machines land within noise of each other. Which one to reach for is therefore not about accuracy:

Decision Default Why Watch out for
Accuracy on curved data Either kernel machine Measured: 2.651 and 2.695 against linear ridge's 3.440. A statistical tie with each other. On data that is genuinely linear, the kernel buys nothing and costs tuning: article 8's lesson transfers.
Model must stay small SVR 125 of 294 cars at the sweep's best; predictions touch only support vectors. Sparsity depends on ε: squeeze the tube and 252 cars are back on the payroll.
Refitting constantly Kernel ridge Closed form: this page re-solves it live per slider tick. No convergence, no initialisation. The solve is O(n³) and the kernel matrix O(n²): at big n, neither fits. BIRCH-style thinking returns.
You need uncertainty The GP costume Same curve, measured to 10-12: but only the Bayesian reading hands you the bands. The bands trust the kernel: article 4's caveats apply unchanged.
Outliers in y SVR's linear ramps Outside the tube the loss grows linearly, not quadratically: a robustness echo of article 3's Huber. ε does not remove leverage in x; RANSAC thinking still applies there.

With that, every chip in the regression branch is lit. This branch opened with a straight line through these same cars; these two machines agree the line should curve, disagree about who pays for errors, and between them contain most of the ideas the branch taught: penalties, kernels, margins, robustness, closed forms and honest priors. One road out of this branch stays unwalked here, and it abandons distance entirely to ask one yes-or-no question at a time instead: Gradient Boosting. The kernel itself comes back later on the classification side, where kernel discriminant analysis gives Fisher's discriminant the same upgrade this article gave ridge.
