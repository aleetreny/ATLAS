Features Before Learning

How vision worked when the representation was designed by hand: gradients, histograms, and invariance you can actually measure

aleetreny

Module 1.3 · Computer Vision · Classical features

Every model in the seventeen articles before this one was handed a table: rows of measurements somebody had already decided were worth taking. Horsepower. Tumour radius. Elevation. An image arrives with no such courtesy. It is a rectangle of brightness values, and the small photograph in this article is already 16,384 numbers for a subject that a person describes in four words.


Worse than the size is the fragility. Shift the picture one pixel to the right and every single number changes, while the thing depicted does not. Rotate it, dim the lights, step back a metre: the pixels are unrecognisable to a model that reads them directly, and the content is identical. A classifier fed raw pixels is being asked to learn, from scratch and from examples, that the world does not change when the camera moves.


For roughly a decade, the answer was to design the middle step by hand. Turn the rectangle of brightness into a shorter list of numbers built to survive exactly the changes that do not matter, then hand that list to one of the classifiers this atlas has already built. This article rebuilds the most influential of those designs, HOG, from pixels, live in your browser and checked against scikit-image to seven decimal places. Then it does the thing the textbooks tend to skip: rather than asserting that SIFT and ORB are invariant, it measures them, against a ground truth that exists because we chose the transformation ourselves.

From Pixels to a Description

The photograph is Eileen Collins, the first woman to command a Space Shuttle, in the standard test image every vision library ships. Watch it stop being pixels.

The Rectangle of Numbers

128 by 128 brightness values. Nothing in here is a shoulder or a helmet; there are only numbers, and a model reading them directly has to discover on its own that neighbouring numbers are related at all.

Only Changes Matter

First move: throw away brightness and keep its rate of change. Each pixel gets the difference between its neighbours, horizontally and vertically. Flat regions go black, edges light up, and an image taken in dimmer light produces almost the same picture, which is the first invariance won.

Chop It Into Cells

Second move: stop caring exactly where inside a small neighbourhood something happened. The image is divided into cells, and from here on, everything that happens inside one cell is pooled. A gradient that shifts by three pixels but stays in its cell changes nothing at all.

Count Orientations, Not Positions

Third move: inside each cell, sort the gradients by direction into nine bins, each weighted by how strong it is. Sixty-four pixels become 9 numbers that say "this patch is mostly one edge direction", with no memory of where in the patch that edge sat. The line under the histogram reads the answer straight off the bars. Note that directions are taken mod 180: a dark-to-light edge and its light-to-dark twin are the same structure.

The Descriptor

Every cell drawn as a rose of oriented lines, each line as long as its bin is heavy. This is the whole of HOG, and a person can still read the photograph in it: shoulders, helmet rim, the curve of the arm. Whatever a classifier needs is evidently still in there.

Two Dials, and a Surprise

HOG has two decisions in it: how big a cell is, and how finely orientation is chopped. Both are yours below, and the descriptor is recomputed from the actual pixels of that photograph on every move, by this page, in JavaScript. It is scikit-image's algorithm rather than a lookalike: same gradient convention, same unsigned binning, same cell windows, verified against the library to 1.2 × 10-7 and re-checked in your browser at load time.


Watch the ratio in the readout as you shrink the cells. HOG is routinely introduced as a compression, and at 16-pixel cells it is one: 1,764 numbers standing in for 16,384 pixels. At four-pixel cells the same descriptor comes out at 34,596 numbers, twice as many as the image had pixels, because HOG normalises over 2×2 blocks of cells that overlap, so every cell is written down four times. The purpose was never to save space. It was to make the numbers stop changing when the picture moves.

Invariance Is a Claim. Measure It.

HOG describes a whole image window. The other classical tradition describes points: find a few hundred spots distinctive enough to be findable again, describe the neighbourhood of each, and recognise an object by how many of its descriptions reappear. SIFT (1999, patent expired in 2020) is the canonical version and ORB (2011) the fast free one built for phones and robots, describing each point as a string of bits so that comparing two of them is a count of differing bits.


Both are advertised as invariant to rotation and scale. That is a testable claim, and testing it needs a ground truth, so the experiment creates one: the image is warped by a similarity transform we chose, which means the correct partner of every keypoint is known in advance. A proposed match counts as correct when the warped position of the first keypoint lands within 3 pixels of its partner. Everything else is a mistake, no matter how confident the matcher was.


The third button is the control that makes the measurement mean something. It takes the raw 16×16 patch of pixels around the very same SIFT keypoints, mean-centred and normalised so brightness alone cannot win, and matches those. Same detector, same points, no clever descriptor. Whatever separates the two curves is the descriptor's doing and nothing else.

Three things in those curves deserve saying out loud. First, the control does not degrade, it dies: at 30 degrees the raw-patch descriptor proposes ten matches and gets zero right, having scored a perfect 472 out of 472 on the unrotated image. Second, SIFT holds a precision of 0.98 or better across every angle measured, and ORB drifts from 1.00 down to 0.72: real invariance, unequally engineered, and the cheaper descriptor pays for its speed in accuracy at large rotations.


Third, the honest artefact: both methods spike at 90 and 180 degrees. That is not a property of the descriptors. Rotating an image by a multiple of a right angle maps the pixel grid exactly onto itself, so no interpolation happens and no blur is introduced, while every other angle resamples and softens the picture a little. The spikes measure the warp, not the method. An experiment that had only tested 90 degrees would have reported invariance that was partly the arithmetic of the test.

Does It Pay?

A design is worth what it buys. The test is deliberately blunt: the same linear support vector machine from module 1.2, the same 10,000 handwritten digits to train on and 5,000 to test, and only the representation changed. Raw pixels give the classifier 784 numbers per image and score 91.16%. HOG gives it 1,296 numbers, computed from those same pixels with no new information from anywhere, and scores 97.32%.


Read that as errors rather than accuracy, which is the reading that matters: 8.84% wrong becomes 2.68% wrong. Seven of every ten mistakes disappear, and the classifier never changed. The entire gain came from a preprocessing step designed by a person who knew that orientation matters and exact position does not. That is what the decade before deep learning looked like, and it is why the arrival of a model that could learn this step for itself was such an earthquake.

Representation Numbers per image Test accuracy What it keeps, and what it throws away
Raw pixels 784 0.9116 Everything, indiscriminately: brightness, position, noise. A one-pixel shift is a different input.

The Verdict

What to reach for, when the representation is still yours to choose.

Situation Default Why Watch out for
Describing a whole window HOG Measured here: 70% of a linear classifier's errors removed by the representation alone. Not a compression. At small cells it produces twice as many numbers as there were pixels.
Matching points between images SIFT when accuracy rules Precision 0.98 or better at every rotation measured, and 0.96 at half scale. 128 floats per keypoint and a slower detector: on this page, roughly twice ORB's cost.
Matching on a budget ORB Binary descriptor, matched by counting differing bits, and it held 0.85 precision at 90 degrees. Precision drifts to 0.72 at large rotations, and the keypoint count collapses under heavy scaling.
Tempted to just compare pixels Do not Perfect on an unchanged image, zero correct matches after a 30-degree turn. The cliff is not gentle. It looks like it works right up until the camera moves, which is the worst possible failure shape.
Reporting invariance Measure it against a known transform Ground truth is free when you choose the warp yourself, and it turns a slogan into a curve. Test more than right angles: rotating by 90 degrees skips interpolation and flatters everything.

There is a limitation in all of this that no amount of engineering fixed. HOG was designed by people who already knew that pedestrians have vertical edges; SIFT was designed by someone who knew that corners survive viewpoint changes. Every one of these descriptors encodes a human hypothesis about what matters, and when the hypothesis is wrong the pipeline has no way to notice, because nothing in it learns. Fixing that is the whole of the rest of this branch.


The next article stays in the hand-designed world but changes the question from "what is in this image" to "where is the face", and answers it fast enough for a camera from 2001, with an idea about arithmetic that is genuinely beautiful. Then the ground moves: a model that designs its own features, measured on the very same digits benchmarked above.
