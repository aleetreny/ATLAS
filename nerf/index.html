A still life photographed from a couple of dozen places, and never from here. Two ways to answer that, the same integral underneath both of them, and both of them running in this page

Module 1.3 · Computer Vision · Neural Rendering

Every article in this module so far has worked on a flat picture. A classifier reads one, a detector puts a box on one, the previous article decided which of its pixels belong to what. But a picture is a projection of something, and the something is where the information went missing: a photograph cannot tell you what is behind the sphere, because nothing behind the sphere reached the sensor.


So the question changes. Given a handful of photographs of a scene, can you produce the photograph nobody took? Not a plausible one. The right one, checkable against the truth.

A Pixel Is An Integral

Forget learning for a moment. Suppose you already had the scene, written down as two functions of a point in space: how much stuff is there, \(\sigma(\mathbf{x})\), and what colour it emits towards you, \(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d})\). A pixel is then a line integral along the ray through it, and the only subtlety is that things in front hide things behind:

\( C(\mathbf{r}) = \displaystyle\int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\, \sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\, \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t), \mathbf{d})\, dt, \qquad T(t) = \exp\!\left(-\displaystyle\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\, ds\right) \)

\(T\) is the transmittance: the fraction of light from depth \(t\) that survives everything in front of it. Scroll through the four quantities on one ray of one of those photographs.

One ray

The ray leaves the camera, crosses the box the scene lives in between \(t_n\) and \(t_f\), and everything that happens to the pixel happens on this segment.

Density

\(\sigma\) along the ray. Empty for most of the way, then a wall where the ray meets a solid. Nothing here is a surface: a surface is what a tall narrow \(\sigma\) looks like from far enough away.

Transmittance

\(T\) starts at one and is destroyed by whatever the ray has already passed through. Once it reaches zero the rest of the ray cannot be seen, which is occlusion falling out of the arithmetic rather than being coded up.

Weight

The product \(T(t)\,\sigma(t)\) is the weight the pixel gives to depth \(t\). It is large only where there is something and nothing in front of it, so it is a spike at the first surface. The pixel is the weighted average of the colour along the ray.

A computer cannot do that integral, so it does a sum. Split the segment into \(N\) pieces, take the density at the middle of each, and the integral becomes the thing every renderer on this page actually computes:

\( \hat{C} = \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\, \alpha_i\, \mathbf{c}_i, \qquad \alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}, \qquad T_i = \displaystyle\prod_{j\,<\,i} (1 - \alpha_j) \)

Which is alpha compositing, arrived at from physics rather than from graphics. Click any pixel and take the sum apart; then take steps away and watch the answer stop being the answer.

The left panel is the reason, and it needs no training to see: how far apart two points are in the encoding, as a function of how far apart they are in the scene. With no bands that is the separation itself, so two points inside one pixel arrive at the network almost identical and it has to work hard to say different things about them. Each band added bends that curve up until it saturates within a fraction of a pixel, after which the network is free. Drag \(L\) and read the two panels together.

A Scene Made Of Blobs

Measured below.


Each blob is an ellipsoid: a centre, a covariance, an opacity and a colour. Projecting one to the screen is a linear map, and the projected shape stays a gaussian, which is the entire trick:

\( \Sigma = R\,S\,S^{\top}R^{\top}, \qquad \Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^{\top}J^{\top} \)

\(W\) puts the world in the camera's frame and \(J\) is the linearised perspective divide at the blob's centre. Sort the results by depth, composite them front to back with the same \(\alpha\) rule as before, and that is the renderer. Drag to orbit, read the third frame to see what the picture is made of, and then take blobs away with the slider until it stops being a picture.

The Verdict

What each idea on this page is worth, at this scale, on this scene.

Idea What it buys Measured here Watch out for
Volume rendering A differentiable path from a scene to a pixel, with occlusion for free Measured above. It is a quadrature rule before it is anything else. Too few steps and you are fitting a model to compensate for your own integrator.
Fourier features A coordinate network that can hold an edge instead of smearing it Measured above. More bands is also more weights, so the honest control is a network with the same budget spent on width. And the ladder saturates, so how many bands to use is a property of the scene rather than a constant to copy.
A field, not a mesh A whole scene, view dependence included, in one small array of weights Measured above. Every pixel is an integral, so a frame costs one network evaluation per sample per pixel. Empty space skipping is not an optimisation here, it is the difference between interactive and not.
Explicit gaussians The same equation over primitives that can be culled, sorted and rasterised Measured above. The blobs are only ever constrained where a photograph looked, and nothing stops one from being any shape at all in a direction nobody stood in. The depth error below the photographed band is where that shows up as a number rather than as a worry.
