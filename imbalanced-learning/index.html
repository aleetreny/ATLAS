Imbalanced Learning

Ninety-nine percent accurate and completely useless: hunting 1,099 trees across 109,880 cells of forest

aleetreny

Module 1.2 · Classification · Imbalance

Roosevelt National Forest, Colorado, mapped as 581,012 cells of thirty by thirty metres, each one labelled with the tree species that dominates it. The rarest label in the entire forest is cover type 4, cottonwood and willow: 2,747 cells, or 0.47% of everything. It is a riparian species, and it will matter later that it only grows low and close to water.


The task of this article is the task of every fraud team, tumour screen and defect detector: find the rare thing. To keep every experiment on this page under a minute, the majority classes are thinned to make the prevalence a round 1%, keeping all 2,747 positives among 274,700 cells. Nothing about the phenomenon changes; only the wait does. A 60/40 split leaves 164,820 cells for training (1,648 positive) and 109,880 for the test set, of which 1,099 are the tree we are hunting.


Every habit this atlas has built so far was formed on balanced problems, and on a problem like this one the habits turn treacherous. Accuracy becomes a lie. The default decision threshold becomes a policy nobody chose. And the most popular fixes, the resampling tricks with the memorable names, buy their headline number by quietly breaking something this atlas has spent the whole classification branch learning to care about. All of it is measured here, on this forest, with the repo's own from-scratch samplers.

The Accuracy Trap

Before any model, look at the problem. Each dot below stands for roughly eleven cells of the held-out test set; the teal ones are the cottonwood stands. All the numbers are the real ones.

One in a Hundred

This is what 1% prevalence looks like: a hundred teal dots adrift in ten thousand. Cover your screen with your palm almost anywhere and you cover no tree at all. Any model that answers this question spends nearly all of its life looking at grey.

The Laziest Possible Model

A classifier that answers "not the tree" every single time, for every cell, scores 99.00% accuracy. It finds none of the 1,099 stands. Zero. The score that graded every classifier in this module so far just awarded a near-perfect grade to a rule that never once did the only job it was given.

A Real Model Barely Moves It

Logistic regression on all 54 terrain features, read at the default 0.5 threshold: 99.33% accurate. A third of a point above never-say-tree. Yet inside that sliver the picture changed completely: 576 stands found, 523 still missed, 216 false alarms. Accuracy compressed all of that into nothing.

Ask Two Questions Instead

Precision: when it says tree, how often is it right? 576 of 792 alarms are real: 72.7%. Recall: of the real trees, how many did it find? 576 of 1,099: 52.4%. Both are about the rare class only, so neither can be bribed by the grey ocean. Every serious decision on this page is a negotiation between these two.

Nothing About 0.5 Is Sacred

The model does not output "tree" or "not tree". It outputs a probability, and 0.5 is just the cut nobody chose. Slide the cut down and recall climbs while precision falls; slide it up and the reverse. The whole trade is yours to pick, and the next section hands you the dial.

One Model, Sixty-Three Thresholds

The same fitted logistic, the same 109,880 test cells, and only the decision cut moving. At \(t = 0.005\) it becomes a screening tool: 1,097 of 1,099 trees caught, at the price of 5,203 false alarms, roughly five wasted field checks for every real find. At \(t = 0.95\) it becomes a sniper: every one of its 25 alarms is a real tree, and 1,074 stands go unfound. The F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, peaks at \(t = 0.258\) with 0.683. None of these is "the correct" threshold. Screening for tumours wants one end; triggering expensive audits wants the other. The dataset cannot make that call, only the cost of each mistake can.

threshold t = 0.5

Two Curves, Two Very Different Stories

Sweep the threshold from one end to the other and you trace a curve. The classical one is ROC: recall (how many trees found) against the false positive rate (false alarms as a share of all 108,781 negatives). Summarised by the area under it, this model scores AUROC 0.996. Frame it and hang it on the wall.


Now the same predictions on the precision-recall plane: AUPRC 0.723. Same model, same test set, same sweep. The difference is the denominator. At the threshold where recall hits 0.99, there are 4,397 false alarms: divided by 108,781 negatives that is a false positive rate of 0.04, a barely visible nudge on the ROC's x-axis. Divided by the 5,486 alarms actually raised, it means only one alarm in five is a real tree, and the PR curve says so out loud. ROC measures the ocean; PR measures your alarm queue. When the classes are imbalanced, the queue is what you live with, and the near-perfect AUROC is how imbalanced models get shipped.

threshold t = 0.5

Making the Rare Thing Common

The oldest instinct on an imbalanced problem is to fix the data instead of the threshold: feed the model a diet where the rare class is not rare. Undersampling throws away negatives until the classes match. Oversampling photocopies the positives. And SMOTE, the famous one, draws new synthetic positives along the straight lines between a real positive and its nearest positive neighbours, on the theory that between two cottonwood stands lies more cottonwood habitat.


Below is what that actually looks like, on the two features that matter most for a riparian tree: elevation and distance to the nearest water. The positives huddle low and wet, between 1,996 and 2,520 metres and never more than 543 metres from water. Watch where each method puts its extra mass, because the geometry is the honest part: photocopies add weight and zero information, while SMOTE's segments genuinely fill the habitat band, and also cheerfully cut through regions where the grey majority lives, planting synthetic trees on dry ground. Every synthetic point here is generated live by this page's own SMOTE, the same recipe as the repo's ScratchSMOTE.

Six Interventions, One Honest Benchmark

Same logistic regression, same 109,880-cell test set, six training diets: untouched data, class weights (each rare mistake costs 99 common ones), random undersampling, random oversampling, SMOTE and ADASYN. The scratch SMOTE was cross-checked against the imbalanced-learn library's: their AUPRC differs by 0.0012, different random draws of the same recipe. Pick a metric and watch the story change.


Read the metrics in order and a pattern appears. At the fixed 0.5 threshold, every intervention sends recall from 0.52 to above 0.99, which looks like a miracle. Precision pays for all of it, collapsing from 0.73 to roughly 0.22. But the ranking metrics barely move, and AUPRC actually drops under every single intervention. The miracle was an illusion of the threshold: rebalancing the training set teaches the model the rare class is common, which shifts every probability upward, which is just the previous widget's slider wearing a disguise. The recall you bought with SMOTE at 0.5 was available from the untouched model at \(t = 0.01\), for free, without retraining.

What Resampling Silently Destroys

There is one place the damage is not subtle. The probabilistic classification article made the case that a good classifier's probabilities should mean something: of the cells given 30%, about 30% should really be the tree. The chart below tests that promise, method by method, by binning the test predictions and comparing what was claimed against what was true.


The untouched baseline keeps its word almost perfectly: it reports an average probability of 0.97% in a world where the true rate is 1.00%, and its confident calls come true. Every intervention breaks the promise the same way and for the same reason: a model trained at 50% prevalence believes the world is half cottonwood. Class weights report an average probability of 4.2%; the resamplers, 4.0% to 4.5%. Four times reality. Worse, the confident calls rot: of the cells class weights marks as 90%+ certain, only 41% are actually the tree. The Brier score, squared error on probabilities, comes out five to six times worse for every intervention than for the baseline.


This is the quiet cost nobody's tutorial mentions: resampling converts a well-calibrated model into a confident liar, in exchange for a threshold move you could have made honestly. If you only ever consume the yes/no decision at one fixed operating point, that may be a price worth paying. The moment anything downstream reads the probability itself, an expected cost, a triage queue, a risk report, it is not.

The Verdict

The benchmark's lesson, condensed into the decisions you actually face.

Situation Do this Why Watch out for
Reporting results Precision, recall and AUPRC, never accuracy alone Accuracy gave 99.00% to a model that found nothing. AUPRC is the summary that felt the imbalance. AUROC of 0.996 on this very page describes the same model as AUPRC 0.723.
You need more recall Move the threshold first Free, instant, honest: t = 0.01 gives recall 0.99 from the untouched model, no retraining. Someone still has to choose the operating point, and that is a cost decision, not a data one.
Tempted by resampling Know what you are buying Measured here: AUPRC dropped under all five interventions (0.72 to 0.60-0.67). The recall jump was an implicit threshold shift. Stronger claims exist for weak learners on tiny minorities; for a linear model on 165k rows, nothing was gained.
Anything reads the probabilities Do not resample, or recalibrate after Every intervention reported a ~4% base rate in a 1% world and Brier got five to six times worse. The damage is invisible in recall and AUROC, the two numbers most tutorials print.
Training cost hurts Undersampling, eyes open 3,296 rows instead of 164,820: it fitted in 0.03s against the baseline's 0.6s and SMOTE's 2.28s. It pays with the biggest variance: here it cost 0.11 of AUPRC against the baseline.

One loose end remains, and it is the biggest one. The baseline's probabilities survived this article because logistic regression was left alone on data whose prevalence was real. But the five broken models are not hopeless: a model that is consistently four times too confident is wrong in a fixable way. Repairing probabilities after the fact, with a held-out set and two classical tools, is the entire subject of the next article, which closes this module by finishing the story these reliability curves started.
