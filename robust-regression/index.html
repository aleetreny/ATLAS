Robust Regression

When one point owns the model: Huber, Theil-Sen and RANSAC

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression

Ten people are drinking in a bar. The average net worth is maybe forty thousand. A billionaire walks in, and the average net worth of the room becomes a hundred million. Every single person in that bar is now, on paper, extraordinarily wealthy. The statistic is arithmetically perfect and completely useless.


Least squares has this problem built into its foundations, and worse. In the first article we accepted squaring the errors without much argument: it makes big mistakes hurt more, it keeps the maths differentiable, and it hands us a closed-form solution. All true. Here is the bill for that convenience. A residual of 10 costs 100. A residual of 100 costs 10,000. There is no ceiling, so the further a point sits from the line, the more absolute authority it has over where that line goes. One observation can outvote a thousand.


That is not a hypothetical. Let's start with a real measurement, of real stars, where least squares reports something that is not merely inaccurate but physically backwards.

Forty-Seven Stars

In 1987 Peter Rousseeuw and Annick Leroy published a table of 47 stars from the CYG OB1 cluster in the constellation Cygnus [1]. For each star: the logarithm of its surface temperature, and the logarithm of how much light it puts out. Astronomers have known the shape of this relationship since 1911. Hotter stars burn brighter. The plot of one against the other is the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, and its main diagonal band is called the main sequence.

The Measurements

Forty-seven stars. Temperature increases to the right, brightness upward. Take a moment before any model touches this: which way does the relationship run, and are all forty-seven points telling the same story?

What Least Squares Concludes

Here is the line that minimizes the sum of squared errors. Its slope is negative. Taken at face value this says that across this cluster, the hotter a star burns, the less light it emits. That is not a small error. It is the opposite of stellar physics.

The Culprits

Four stars, highlighted. They are cool and enormously bright, which puts them in the upper left, far from everyone else. They are not measurement errors. They are red giants: real, well-understood stars that have left the main sequence and are burning helium in a swollen shell.

Four Against Forty-Three

Drop those four and refit. The slope flips from −0.41 to +2.05, and the line finally lies along the band the other forty-three stars form. Four points out of forty-seven, under nine percent of the sample, were not just biasing the answer. They were reversing it.

Why They Win

It is not because their errors are large. It is because of where they sit on the x axis. A point far out along x acts on the line like a long lever: a small movement at that end swings the whole thing. Statisticians call this quantity leverage, and it is the real villain of this article.

Robust Estimators

Two methods we are about to build, Theil-Sen and RANSAC, recover a positive slope without being told which stars are giants. They find the main sequence on their own. Getting there requires deciding what to do with a point you distrust: dampen it, out-vote it, or expel it.

The Price of a Mistake

Every regression method is an opinion about how much a mistake should cost, and that opinion is the loss function. Fitting a line means choosing the line that makes total cost smallest, so the shape of the cost curve decides whose complaints get listened to.


Three candidates. Squared loss, what OLS uses:

$$ L_{\text{sq}}(r) = \tfrac{1}{2} r^{2} $$

Absolute loss, which charges the same rate forever and gives you median regression:

$$ L_{\text{abs}}(r) = |r| $$

And Huber loss, which is quadratic near zero and switches to linear once a residual crosses a threshold \(\delta\), so it keeps the smooth, efficient behaviour of least squares for ordinary points and stops escalating for extreme ones [2]:

$$ L_{\delta}(r) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2}r^{2} & |r| \le \delta \\[4pt] \delta\left(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right) & |r| > \delta \end{cases} $$


The loss curve is not the interesting picture though. What actually decides the fit is its derivative, because that is the force each point exerts while the optimizer searches. Look at the right panel: under squared loss the pull grows without limit, so a point far enough away can overpower any number of well-behaved ones. Under absolute and Huber loss the pull is capped. That cap is the whole idea of robustness in one line.

δ = 1.35

The default δ = 1.35 is not arbitrary. Measured in units of the noise standard deviation, it is the value at which Huber retains 95% of the efficiency of least squares when the data really is Gaussian, which is the price you pay for the insurance.

Not All Outliers Are Equal

Here is the distinction that decides which method you need, and the one the word "outlier" hides. Take a clean cloud of points and move a single one of them somewhere strange. Where you move it matters more than how far.


Move it straight up, into the middle of the x range: a vertical outlier. It has a big residual and it pulls the line, but it sits where plenty of other points can argue back. Now drag it far out to the right instead: a leverage point. Out there it has no neighbours, so nothing contradicts it, and the line pivots to follow. The formal measure is the hat value, which for a single predictor is

$$ h_i = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_i - \bar{x})^2}{\sum_j (x_j - \bar{x})^2} $$

and notice what is missing from it: \(y\). Leverage is decided entirely by where a point sits along x, before anyone looks at whether its outcome is surprising. A point above the conventional cutoff of \(2p/n\) has disproportionate say by construction.


Drag the large point below and watch all four methods react. Then find the position that breaks each one.

The dashed grey line is the fit on the clean points alone, which is the answer every method should be trying to recover.

Park the point high above the middle and every robust method shrugs, while OLS visibly tilts. A large residual in a crowded region is the easy case: it is exactly what Huber is built to discount, and there are neighbours to contradict it.


Now drag it far to the right. OLS breaks immediately. Huber bends but mostly survives, which is worth being precise about, because the textbook line that "Huber has breakdown point zero" makes you expect a collapse that does not happen here. Then press give it two companions and watch Huber go over the edge with them.


That is the real mechanism, and the star data measures it exactly. Adding the red giants back one at a time, Huber's slope runs +2.55, +1.70, +0.94, −0.08, −0.44. It is not that Huber is immune to leverage and then suddenly is not. It is being dragged the entire time, and by the third giant the sign has flipped. Huber decides who to distrust by looking at residuals, and a group of leverage points drags the line onto itself, so their residuals stay small. They never look suspicious. They look like the best-behaved points on the chart.


Hence the formal result: the breakdown point of Huber regression, the smallest fraction of contamination that can send the estimate arbitrarily far, is zero. That is a worst-case statement about adversarially placed points, not a promise that any single outlier will wreck it. What you should take from it is that Huber offers no guarantee against leverage, and on real data the margin ran out at three points in forty-seven.

Out-Voting the Outliers

If the mean is the fragile statistic and the median is the sturdy one, the obvious move is to build a regression out of medians. That is exactly what Theil and Sen did [3], and the recipe is short enough to state in one breath: take every pair of points, compute the slope of the line through that pair, and report the median of all those slopes.

$$ \hat{m} = \operatorname{median}_{i

No threshold, no learning rate, no loss function to tune. Below, every one of the pairwise slopes for the star data is drawn as a tick. Pairs that involve a red giant are marked: notice they are scattered off in their own territory, they are numerous, and the median sits calmly among the honest majority regardless.

There is a catch, and it is a beautiful piece of arithmetic. A slope needs two points, so contamination compounds. If a fraction \(\varepsilon\) of your points are bad, the fraction of pairs touched by at least one bad point is \(1 - (1-\varepsilon)^2\). The median only survives while fewer than half the pairs are contaminated:

$$ 1 - (1-\varepsilon)^2 < \tfrac{1}{2} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \varepsilon < 1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 29.3\% $$

So Theil-Sen tolerates just under thirty percent contamination, and no more. That is far better than the zero percent Huber offers against leverage, and it comes with no knobs at all. The cost is speed: every pair means \(\binom{n}{2}\) slopes, so a hundred thousand points would mean five billion of them.


A trap worth knowing about, found while checking this page's numbers against a reference implementation. Two different estimators answer to the name Theil-Sen. The classic one is the median of pairwise slopes defined above, which is what scipy.stats.theilslopes computes and what runs on this page. Scikit-learn's TheilSenRegressor is something else: it takes the spatial median of least squares fits over random subsamples, which generalises to many features but is not the same quantity in one dimension. On these 47 stars the two disagree by a wide margin, 1.73 against 1.49. Both are defensible robust estimators. Only one matches the formula above.

Guess, Then Take a Vote

RANSAC comes at it from a completely different direction, one that feels more like gambling than statistics [4]. Rather than fit all the data carefully, fit almost none of it, repeatedly:


  1. Hypothesize. Pick the smallest sample that defines a model. For a line, two points.
  2. Verify. Ask every other point whether it lies within a threshold of that line.
  3. Count. The agreeing points are the consensus set. Its size is this guess's score.
  4. Repeat. Hundreds of times, keeping the best-scoring guess.
  5. Refine. Refit properly, using every member of the winning consensus.

The logic is that two honest points define a line that many other honest points will sit near, while a line drawn through a giant agrees with almost nobody. Watch it search the star data. Each frame is one hypothesis: the two sampled stars are ringed, the candidate line is drawn, and the stars that voted for it turn solid.

threshold ε = 0.60

Two things are worth noticing. First, RANSAC does not merely fit a line, it labels the data: the consensus set is a by-product you can inspect, and on the star data it separates the main sequence from the giants without ever being told they exist. Second, the threshold \(\varepsilon\) is doing real work. Set it too tight and no hypothesis gathers a majority; too loose and the giants are welcomed back in. Unlike Theil-Sen, this method asks you to know something about your noise.


How many guesses are enough? That has an exact answer. If a fraction \(\varepsilon\) of the data is bad and each hypothesis needs \(s\) points, the probability that one draw is all-clean is \((1-\varepsilon)^s\), so to get at least one clean draw with probability \(p\) you need

$$ N = \frac{\log(1-p)}{\log\left(1 - (1-\varepsilon)^{s}\right)} $$

For a line (\(s = 2\)) with 30% contamination and 99% confidence, that is just 7 iterations. Even at 60% contamination it is only 70. This is why RANSAC keeps working when the data is mostly garbage: it never needs a clean majority, only a clean pair, and often enough. The usual claim that it fails above 50% is a simplification: what actually degrades is the reliability of the consensus score, since a large enough wrong cluster can out-vote the truth.

The Verdict

Every method here answers one question differently: what do you do with a point you do not trust? The right answer depends on a question about your data that no algorithm can answer for you. Are your outliers garbage, or are they loud truth?


A mistyped price is garbage; delete it and never think about it again. A market crash in a returns series is loud truth; it really happened, it should not dominate your model, and it must not be erased. Those two cases call for opposite tools.

Method Does what with a suspect point Breakdown Tuning Reach for it when…
OLS Obeys it, without limit 0% None You have already established the data is clean. It remains the most efficient estimator when it is.
Huber Dampens it, by residual size 0% against leverage δ (in units of σ) Heavy-tailed but honest noise: extreme values that are real and deserve less weight, not deletion.
Theil-Sen Out-votes it, via medians 29.3% None at all The safe default below roughly ten thousand points. Deterministic, tuning-free, and it protects against leverage.
RANSAC Expels it from the model ~50%, threshold permitting ε, iterations Heavy contamination, and you want the outliers identified as output. Standard in computer vision.

One honest correction to close on, because this article was built from a notebook that got it half right. That notebook concluded Huber fails against a dense cluster of structural outliers, and showed a nearly flat fitted line as proof. The conclusion is correct, but the picture was not: it used a fixed δ on unscaled data with an optimizer stopped long before convergence. Rerun properly, scaled and converged, Huber lands at a slope of 21.2 where the truth is 87.4. Still badly dragged, so the lesson stands, but the original demonstration was measuring the optimizer as much as the loss. Every Huber fit on this page uses iteratively reweighted least squares with an estimated scale, for exactly that reason.
