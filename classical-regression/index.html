Classical Regression

The Art of Drawing Lines: OLS, Polynomial Regression, GLMs & Partial Least Squares

aleetreny

Module 1.1 · Regression

Before the era of GPU clusters and billion-parameter models, there was classical statistics. Modern machine learning often treats a model as a black-box optimization problem: whatever maps inputs to outputs with the lowest test error wins. Classical statistics treats data modeling as a scientific inquiry. We don't just ask "what is the prediction?". We ask tougher questions:


  • How exactly does variable \(x\) impact \(y\)? (interpretability)
  • How confident are we that the relationship is real and not noise? (significance)
  • Does the data actually obey the structure we are modeling? (diagnostics)

This split has a name. Leo Breiman famously called it the two cultures of statistical modeling [1]: the data modeling culture assumes reality follows a specific stochastic process and estimates its parameters; the algorithmic culture treats nature as unknown and hunts for the function with the best test error. This article lives in the first culture, and everything that follows rests on one rigid, additive claim about how reality works:

$$ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p + \epsilon $$

The coefficients \(\beta\) are the signal we want to recover. The term \(\epsilon\) is the irreducible error, the noise of the universe. Our entire job is separating one from the other.


But you cannot just throw data at this equation and hope. Valid classical inference requires the famous LINE assumptions: Linearity of the relationship, Independence of observations, Normality of the errors, and Equal variance (homoscedasticity). When they break, your p-values and confidence intervals turn into decoration.


Here is the plan: four techniques, each one relaxing exactly one assumption the previous one couldn't survive without: OLS draws the straight line, Polynomial Regression lets it bend, GLMs free it from Gaussian noise, and PLS rescues it when features outnumber reason. Let's draw some lines.

Ordinary Least Squares

We'll start with a single real question: how does engine power affect fuel efficiency? Below is the classic Auto MPG dataset [2], 392 cars from 1970-1982, each a dot: horsepower on the x-axis, miles-per-gallon on the y-axis. Scroll on, the chart will keep pace with the story.

The Reality

Every dot is a car. The pattern is obvious to the naked eye: more horsepower, fewer miles per gallon. Our goal is to compress this whole cloud into a single equation that predicts MPG from horsepower.

The Dumbest Model

Start stupid on purpose. Ignore horsepower entirely and predict the average MPG (23.4) for every car: \(\hat{y} = \bar{y}\). A 50 HP economy car and a 230 HP muscle car get the same prediction. Clearly bad, but how bad, exactly?

Measuring Badness

Draw the error of each prediction: the vertical gap between every dot and the line. These are the residuals. Square them (so big misses hurt disproportionately), average them, and you get the mean squared error, our score to beat: \(\mathrm{MSE} = 60.8\).

The Best Straight Line

Now let the line tilt and shift, searching for the slope and intercept that make the squared residuals as small as possible. There is exactly one winner, the least squares line: \(\hat{y} = 39.94 - 0.158\,x\). The MSE collapses from 60.8 to 23.9.

Reading the Line

This is why classical statistics loves linear models: the coefficients say things. Slope \(-0.158\): every extra horsepower costs about 0.16 MPG. Intercept \(39.94\): a mythical 0 HP car would get ~40 MPG. Physically absurd, but it anchors the line. And \(R^2 = 0.61\): the line explains 61% of the variation in MPG.

Prediction Is Free

Once fitted, predicting is just plugging in. A 150 HP engine? Follow it up to the line: \(39.94 - 0.158 \times 150 \approx 16.3\) MPG. This is the entire life of a regression model: fit once, then answer questions forever at the cost of one multiplication.

The Flaw

Look closer. The errors are not random. They are systematic. Below ~75 HP and above ~180 HP the dots sit above the line; in the middle they sit below. The cloud is a banana and our model is a ruler. Push far enough right and it predicts negative MPG. The linearity assumption just snapped.

How the Line Is Found

No search, no iterations, no learning rate. For least squares there is a closed-form solution: you compute the best coefficients directly with linear algebra. It's called the Normal Equation:

$$ \hat{\beta} = (X^{\top} X)^{-1} X^{\top} y $$

What is actually happening in there? \(X^{\top}y\) measures the co-movement between each feature and the target, the direction of the relationship. \(X^{\top}X\) captures the variance and overlap of the features, their scale. Inverting it and multiplying is, loosely, "dividing correlation by variance": normalize the co-movement by how spread out the features are, and the coefficients fall out. One detail matters: we prepend a column of 1s to \(X\), otherwise the line is forced through the origin and \(\beta_0\) can't exist.


A note from the trenches: textbooks write \((X^{\top}X)^{-1}\), but production code never inverts the matrix. Solving the linear system directly (np.linalg.solve, QR decomposition) is faster and numerically safer. Same math, better manners.


Enough theory. Grab the line yourself. Drag the two handles to change the intercept and slope, and watch the squared error respond. Then press the button and let the Normal Equation snap it to perfection. You can also flip to residual view: the fastest diagnostic in statistics. If the residuals show a pattern, your model is missing one.

Polynomial Regression

The banana problem: how do you model a curve when your solver only knows how to fit lines? The trick: keep the model linear in its parameters while making it non-linear in its features:

$$ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 $$

To the algorithm, \(x^2\) is just another anonymous column of numbers. It has no idea it came from squaring the first one. Feed it "squared horsepower" and the very same Normal Equation happily learns a parabola. No new algorithm, just feature engineering, vintage 1805.


But bending comes with a temptation. If degree 2 helps, why not 5? Why not 9? This time we're keeping score honestly: the model is fitted on training cars (solid dots) and graded on 25% it never saw (hollow dots). And there is a second, crueler knob: how many cars the model gets to learn from. Flexibility alone rarely kills. Flexibility times data scarcity does. Starve the model and slide the degree up.

degree: 1
training cars: 294

Degree 2 buys almost all of the gain: test \(R^2\) climbs from 0.57 to 0.66, and the curve finally speaks physics: MPG drops fast when you add power to a weak engine, then the drop flattens: diminishing returns. Everything after that is small change, and the widget will tell you exactly how small, because it scores every degree and names the winner rather than taking anyone's word for where the sweet spot is. On all 294 training cars it turns out to be degree 7, at 0.68, and even degree 9 only slips back to 0.65. Abundant data is a straitjacket, the wiggles have nowhere to go. But drop to ~30 cars and push the degree up: the curve starts slaloming through individual points, the train score inflates toward 1, and the test score craters (even below zero, worse than predicting the mean). That gap is overfitting: not flexibility itself, but flexibility the data can no longer discipline. A flexible model plus an honest holdout is the entire immune system of machine learning in one chart.

Generalized Linear Models

Ordinary least squares hides its strongest assumption in plain sight: the noise \(\epsilon\) is Gaussian: symmetric, continuous, unbounded. But look at what we actually predict in the wild:


  • Counts: people at a table, clicks, claims. Integers. Never 2.5, never −1.
  • Strictly positive values: prices, durations, rainfall.
  • Probabilities: bounded between 0 and 1.

Fit a straight line to any of these and it will eventually predict something physically impossible: minus two customers, a 150% probability. Generalized Linear Models fix this with one elegant move, the link function:

$$ g(\mathbb{E}[y]) = X\beta $$

Instead of predicting \(y\) directly, the linear machinery predicts a transformed version of its mean. For count data the recipe is the Poisson GLM: assume \(y \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu)\) and set \(\log(\mu) = X\beta\), so predictions \(\mu = e^{X\beta}\) are positive by construction. The price: no closed form. For the first time in this article we must search for \(\beta\), descending on the negative log-likelihood (the same thing as maximizing likelihood, flipped so it is a cost to minimize like every other one on this page), and the gradient turns out to be an old friend wearing a mask:

$$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}}{\partial \beta} = \tfrac{1}{n} X^{\top}(\underbrace{e^{X\beta}}_{\hat{y}} - \,y) $$

Identical in shape to the OLS gradient, except the prediction inside is exponential. (Many textbooks solve GLMs with Newton-Raphson / IRLS, which converges in a handful of steps; plain gradient descent, shown here, is the same idea with smaller strides.)


The stage: the tips dataset [3], 244 restaurant bills. Given the total bill, predict the party size. Headcount is a count. Watch OLS walk into the trap, then press the buttons to train the Poisson GLM live, one gradient step at a time.

Two things to notice once training converges. First, the shape: the Poisson curve bends gently upward. At $50 bills it expects bigger parties at an accelerating rate, while OLS is stuck on a fixed slope that would happily cross into negative diners left of the axis. Second, toggle the variance envelope. OLS assumes the spread of party sizes is identical for a $5 bill and a $50 bill (constant band). Poisson knows that variance grows with the mean (\(\sigma^2 = \mu\)). Watch what that buys in relative terms: a $5 bill predicts about two diners and OLS hands it exactly the same absolute uncertainty as a $50 one, while Poisson lets the band grow with the prediction, so the spread relative to the prediction falls from 0.74 at the cheap end to 0.45 at the expensive one. The widening funnel isn't a bug, it's the model telling the truth about uncertainty.

Partial Least Squares

One assumption left to break, and this one kills the Normal Equation outright. \((X^{\top}X)^{-1}\) only exists if the features are linearly independent enough to invert. Two ways reality sabotages that:


  • Multicollinearity: features that are near-clones of each other. \(X^{\top}X\) becomes nearly singular and the coefficients explode into nonsense.
  • p > n: more features than samples. Infinitely many perfect solutions, none of them meaningful.

The stage this time: wine chemistry [4], 178 wines from three grape cultivars, each described by 13 lab measurements. Hover the correlation matrix: those deep red and blue blocks are features telling you the same thing twice. Flavanoids and total phenols correlate at +0.86: pay for one, get the other free.

The cure is compression: project 13 noisy, redundant dimensions onto a couple of latent directions that matter. But matter to whom? That question splits two famous algorithms:


  • PCA finds directions of maximum variance of X, never looking at the target. Unsupervised compression.
  • PLS finds directions of maximum covariance between X and y, keeping only the information that helps prediction. Supervised compression.

Under the hood PLS runs a beautifully simple loop called NIPALS: compute the direction of maximum covariance with the target, \(w = X^{\top}y\) (normalized); project the samples onto it, \(t = Xw\); measure how much of \(X\) that direction explains, \(p = X^{\top}t / t^{\top}t\); then deflate, subtracting the explained part, \(X \leftarrow X - t p^{\top}\), and repeat on the leftovers. Each pass strips one layer of signal off the data.


Below, both algorithms compress the same 13-D chemistry into 2-D. Flip the toggle and watch every wine fly from its PCA position to its PLS position. The colors (three cultivars) are shown to you. PCA never saw them, PLS did.

PCA does a respectable job: with this much structure in the chemistry, raw variance alone separates the cultivars decently. On the first axis PLS lands almost exactly where PCA does, correlated with it at 0.999, and that is worth saying out loud rather than hiding: when the loudest variation in \(X\) happens to be the variation that matters, an unsupervised axis is already the right one. The difference is the second component. PCA spends it on whatever variance is left over, which turns out to know nothing about the cultivar. PLS spends it on the label, and its spread collapses from 1.59 to 0.83 while its correlation with the class rises from 0.01 to 0.25. Thirteen correlated measurements, and the predictive essence turns out to be essentially one clever linear combination. That is PLS's entire job description: find the needle of signal in the haystack of redundancy, even when \(X^{\top}X\) is too broken to invert.

The Verdict

Four techniques, four assumptions relaxed, one engine underneath. If you're standing in front of a fresh dataset, this is the decision matrix:

Technique Reach for it when… It relaxes… Solver Watch out for
OLS You need the interpretable baseline: one coefficient = one marginal effect, plus p-values and confidence intervals. nothing (the reference point) Closed form (Normal Equation) Curved relationships, outliers, correlated features.
Polynomial The mean relationship visibly bends but you want to keep linear machinery and closed-form fitting. Linearity (in features) Closed form on engineered features Overfitting at high degree; wild extrapolation beyond the data range.
GLM The target's support isn't the real line: counts (Poisson), yes/no (logistic), skewed positives (Gamma). Normality (via link functions) Iterative MLE (IRLS / gradient) Choosing the wrong family/link; overdispersion in counts.
PLS Features are heavily correlated or outnumber samples, and compression must serve prediction of a specific y. Feature independence Iterative deflation (NIPALS) Choosing the number of components; harder coefficient-level interpretation.

The deeper lesson of classical regression is the habit of mind: state your assumptions, check them against the data, and when one breaks, relax that one, not everything at once. The next article in this module takes the opposite failure seriously: what happens when the model has too much freedom, and we must pay to constrain it. That story is called Regularization.
